Osmotic Blistering: Mechanism, Diagnosis, Repair

Lesson 0001 told you polyester blisters and vinyl ester/epoxy mostly don't. Lesson 0003 told you blister warranties drive resin decisions. Neither told you why a blister actually forms, or what fixing one costs in real time and money — and that's the level of detail that makes you sound like you know the material, not just the marketing.

What's physically happening inside a blister

A laminate is never perfectly solid — hand layup and even infusion leave microscopic voids between fibers and resin.1 Water vapor migrates through gelcoat over years (gelcoat is water-resistant, not waterproof) and collects in those voids. Two things then happen at once:

  • Osmosis pulls in more water. Water reacts with unreacted resin components and byproducts trapped in the void, forming a concentrated, acidic fluid. Because that fluid is more concentrated than the water sitting just outside the laminate, osmotic pressure keeps drawing more water across the gelcoat and into the void — even against the surrounding water pressure of the hull sitting in a marina.
  • Hydrolysis feeds the reaction. That acidic fluid attacks the resin matrix itself, breaking down the ester linkages in cured polyester resin — a reaction called hydrolysis. This produces more acidic byproducts, which pulls in more water, which drives more hydrolysis. It's a self-reinforcing cycle, not a one-time event.1

The pressure builds until the gelcoat bulges outward — the blister you can see and feel — and eventually ruptures, releasing a vinegar-smelling acidic fluid. This is why blistering gets worse the longer a hull sits in the water untreated, and why "small blister now" reliably becomes "bigger repair bill later."

This is exactly the Lesson 0001 trade-off, at the chemistry level. Epoxy resists this whole cycle better than polyester because its cross-links form through an ether linkage, which is chemically more stable against hydrolysis than polyester's ester linkage.1 Vinyl ester sits in between — its backbone is tighter than polyester's, so water penetrates more slowly, but it still uses ester linkages, so it's not immune, just more resistant. That's the real mechanism behind the cost/water-resistance trade-off from Lesson 0001 — not marketing language, actual chemistry.

Diagnosis

Surveyors check for blistering with a combination of a visual/tactile inspection (soft spots, visible bumps, a vinegar smell after popping a test blister) and a moisture meter, which reads the laminate's moisture content as a percentage. A hull with elevated readings is retaining water even before blisters are visible — moisture content, not visible blisters, is the leading indicator.

What repair actually involves — and costs in time

This is the part that surprises people who haven't done it: repair is mostly a drying problem, not a patching problem.2

  1. Remove the gelcoat below the waterline — by grinding, sandblasting, or gelcoat peeling — down to the laminate.
  2. Dry the hull thoroughly. This is the slow part: it can take several days to several weeks, often with forced warm-air circulation, until moisture meter readings drop below roughly 3%. Proceeding before the hull is dry just traps water back in and restarts the whole cycle.2
  3. Fair and seal with epoxy — brush epoxy resin into the exposed laminate (epoxy, not polyester, because of the hydrolysis resistance above), fill with epoxy fairing compound, sand smooth.
  4. Re-gelcoat or paint to match the hull.

A hull can be out of the water for a season for a proper blister repair — that multi-week drying step is non-negotiable and can't be rushed with more labor. This is your strongest prevention argument: a resin choice that avoids blistering in the first place isn't just a materials cost, it's avoiding a haul-out season a customer can't get back.

The catch in the "vinyl ester skin coat + warranty" fix (from Lesson 0003)

Lesson 0003 presented the vinyl ester skin coat as boatbuilders' cheap, effective answer to blister warranties. There's a real-world complication worth knowing before you repeat that pitch to anyone who owns the boat, not just builds it:

"We'll have a polyester boat with a single skin coat of vinylester, and the manufacturer will give a warranty of five or 10 years against blistering, but then they won't let you sand the boat to put bottom paint on it," a source identified only as Potts told Soundings. "A lot [of manufacturers] use a no-sanding primer on top of the gelcoat. It's very touchy. For some boats it works out well, and for others it doesn't."

(This workspace could not confirm Potts's role/affiliation — soundingsonline.com is unreachable from this session's network, same limitation as Lesson 0004's Transport Canada sources. The quote itself is corroborated by two independent search results.)

In some cases the no-sanding primer doesn't last. When it deteriorates, the bond between the gelcoat and the bottom paint breaks down, and the paint peels away. At that point the owner faces a choice: leave it, or sand it — and sanding voids the blister warranty.3

Why this matters for you: the blister warranty isn't a clean, no-downside selling point — it comes with a maintenance restriction most boat owners don't fully understand at purchase time, and that restriction can fail on its own timeline, independent of whether the underlying skin coat is doing its job. If you're pitching a resin/skin-coat system tied to a blister warranty, "what's the bottom-paint compatibility story, and does it hold up over the warranty period?" is a question a sophisticated buyer will eventually ask — better you raise it first.
