Canadian Boat Building Standards for Resin

Lesson 0003 covered what drives a US boat builder's resin choice. If you're selling into the Canadian market — or to a builder who exports there — the compliance picture is different in one important way, and the same in another that's easy to get wrong.

The construction standard: TP 1332

Boats built or sold in Canada fall under the Small Vessel Regulations (SOR/2010-91), made under the Canada Shipping Act, 2001. These regulations point to TP 1332 — Construction Standards for Small Vessels as the baseline construction requirement for pleasure craft and small vessels up to 15 gross tonnage.1

Edition correction. Earlier versions of this lesson cited the 2010 edition of TP 1332 throughout, because that's the version most easily findable via search. Transport Canada's own edition history lists versions from 1974, 1978, 1999, 2002, 2004, 2010, and 2024 — meaning the 2010 text I'd been citing is likely superseded. A file path pointing to a document folder dated November 2023 surfaced during this research, which is almost certainly the newer edition (or the near-final draft that became it) — not the 2010 one. I still can't fetch or read either PDF directly (see below), so I don't know what, if anything, changed between editions. Treat every claim below as possibly describing an outdated edition until someone reads the actual current document.

Here's the part that's easy to get wrong: most Canadian-market builders don't actually build to a Canada-only spec. Transport Canada has a standing policy — formally titled "TIER I – Policy: Acceptance of Alternative Construction Requirements for Small Vessels," effective October 30, 2019 — accepting ABYC (American Boat and Yacht Council) standards as an alternative to TP 1332.2 This is now corroborated by three independent sources (Transport Canada's own search-indexed page, plus trade-press coverage from IIMS and Boating Industry), which is solid enough to say with confidence. ABYC's own Technical VP at the time, Craig Scholten, called it a move toward "one set of standards throughout North America," adding: "Being able to design, certify, and produce product to one standard will streamline and simplify everyone's efforts."4 The policy covers vessels constructed, manufactured, or rebuilt in Canada, or imported into it.

Scope: two tiers, not one blanket swap

The policy isn't a single flat list — it's structured in two tiers by vessel type and size, each with its own accepted-standards table and its own Canadian-modifications table:

  • Appendix 1 — Table 1 (accepted ABYC standards) and Table 2 (Canadian modifications) — covers pleasure craft under 24m and non-pleasure craft up to 6m.
  • Appendix 2 — Table 3 and Table 4 — covers non-pleasure craft over 6m separately.

I have not read either table directly (see the verification note below), but search results consistently associate this policy with specific ABYC standards covering fuel systems (H-24, Gasoline Fuel Systems), ventilation (H-32, boats using diesel fuel), electrical systems (E-11, AC & DC Electrical Systems on Boats), and hull fittings (H-27, Seacocks, Thru-hull Fittings, and Drain Plugs) — all systems/equipment standards, not laminate or resin specifications.

Correcting my own earlier framing. An earlier version of this lesson said Canadian builders build to "the same ABYC laminate/construction standards" as US builders. That overstated it. One source (unconfirmed elsewhere) states plainly that ABYC does not publish a scantling rule — i.e., no ABYC standard specifies hull structural design or laminate/resin lay-up schedules the way a classification society's rules would. If that's accurate, the ABYC-alternative policy harmonizes systems safety (fuel, electrical, ventilation, thru-hull fittings) between the US and Canada, while structural/laminate resin specification for FRP hulls remains governed by TP 1332 itself, not by ABYC. That's a more precise — and more useful — picture for a resin sales conversation: don't expect "ABYC compliance" to be your entry point for a laminate/resin pitch; TP 1332 directly is.

The flame-retardant claim — upgraded, but still not primary-source-read

An earlier search turned up a specific claim: that TP 1332 requires flame-retardant additives in the exposed resin layer within the engine space for FRP hulls, or alternatively that the final hull lay-up layer there must be woven roving. At the time, this rested on one search-engine snippet with no section number and no corroboration, and this lesson said so plainly.

Since then, the identical sentence — same wording, "flame retardant additives should be incorporated in the exposed layer of resin within the engine space or the final layer of hull lay-up should be of woven rovings" — turned up again, verbatim, via a completely independent search (triggered while investigating the newer 2023/2024 edition). Two independent searches surfacing the exact same phrasing is real evidence this is a genuine quote from the document, not a search-summarizer artifact. Upgraded from "unverified" to "corroborated, likely accurate" — but still not confirmed by section number, and still not read from the PDF directly, so keep the same discipline: it's solid enough to ask a builder about, not yet solid enough to assert as settled fact with a section citation attached.

The same "one source, not yet corroborated" caveat still applies to two other claims: the specific ABYC standard numbers (H-24, H-32, E-11, H-27) named earlier as examples under the accepted-standards tables, and the claim that ABYC has no scantling rule. Neither has resurfaced independently the way the flame-retardant claim did.

What's different from the US picture (Lesson 0003). Lesson 0003's regulatory angle was styrene/VOC emissions: the US EPA regulates boat manufacturing plants directly under a federal NESHAP/MACT standard. As of this research, there is no equivalent Canada-wide, boat-manufacturing-specific VOC emissions regulation confirmed — Canada's existing VOC Concentration Limits regulations cover other product categories (architectural coatings, automotive refinishing), and it's unclear from public sources whether or how they extend to fiberglass boat resin.3 Do not repeat the US emissions-compliance pitch verbatim to a Canadian builder — the Canadian compliance conversation centers on TP 1332/ABYC construction and safety compliance (and provincial air-quality rules, which vary and aren't covered here), not a confirmed federal emissions mandate.
