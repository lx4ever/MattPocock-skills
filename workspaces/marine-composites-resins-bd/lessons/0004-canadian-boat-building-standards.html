Canadian Boat Building Standards for Resin

Lesson 0003 covered what drives a US boat builder's resin choice. If you're selling into the Canadian market — or to a builder who exports there — the compliance picture is different in one important way, and the same in another that's easy to get wrong.

The construction standard: TP 1332

Boats built or sold in Canada fall under the Small Vessel Regulations (SOR/2010-91), made under the Canada Shipping Act, 2001. These regulations point to TP 1332 — Construction Standards for Small Vessels (Transport Canada, 2010 edition) as the baseline construction requirement for pleasure craft and small vessels up to 15 gross tonnage.1

Here's the part that's easy to get wrong: most Canadian-market builders don't actually build to a Canada-only spec. Transport Canada has a standing policy — formally titled "TIER I – Policy: Acceptance of Alternative Construction Requirements for Small Vessels," effective October 30, 2019 — accepting ABYC (American Boat and Yacht Council) standards as an alternative to TP 1332, for pleasure craft under 24m and non-pleasure craft up to 6m.2 The policy isn't a blanket swap: its Appendix 1 lists which specific ABYC standards qualify (Table 1) alongside required Canadian modifications to each one (Table 2). In practice, that means many Canadian builders are building to the same ABYC laminate/construction standards as their US counterparts, with a defined set of Canada-specific tweaks layered on top — not a wholly separate resin requirement.

A claim I have not been able to verify directly — read this before you use it

One earlier search turned up a specific claim: that TP 1332 requires flame-retardant additives in the exposed resin layer within the engine space for FRP hulls, or alternatively that the final hull lay-up layer there must be woven roving. I have not been able to confirm this against the source document. Every Transport Canada domain, plus the mirrors I tried (Justice Canada's regulation text, an archived copy, a third-party document host), is blocked by this session's network policy — I could not fetch the PDF, the HTML version, or any cached copy of it. The claim rests on a single search-engine summary snippet, with no section number and no independent corroboration.

Do not repeat this specific claim to a customer as fact. It's plausible — flame-retardant requirements near engine spaces are common in small-vessel standards generally — but "plausible and found once" is not the bar for something you'll say out loud to a boat builder who may know TP 1332 better than you do. Get the actual PDF (or the current text at the link below) and confirm the exact wording and section number before this goes anywhere near a sales conversation.

What's different from the US picture (Lesson 0003). Lesson 0003's regulatory angle was styrene/VOC emissions: the US EPA regulates boat manufacturing plants directly under a federal NESHAP/MACT standard. As of this research, there is no equivalent Canada-wide, boat-manufacturing-specific VOC emissions regulation confirmed — Canada's existing VOC Concentration Limits regulations cover other product categories (architectural coatings, automotive refinishing), and it's unclear from public sources whether or how they extend to fiberglass boat resin.3 Do not repeat the US emissions-compliance pitch verbatim to a Canadian builder — the Canadian compliance conversation centers on TP 1332/ABYC construction and safety compliance (and provincial air-quality rules, which vary and aren't covered here), not a confirmed federal emissions mandate.
