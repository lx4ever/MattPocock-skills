Osmotic Blistering Cheat Sheet

The compressed version of Lesson 0005.

The cycle, in order

  1. Microscopic voids in the laminate trap water vapor that migrates through gelcoat over years
  2. Water reacts with resin byproducts in the void, forming a concentrated acidic fluid
  3. Osmotic pressure pulls in more water because that fluid is more concentrated than water outside the hull
  4. Hydrolysis: the acidic fluid breaks down polyester's ester linkages, producing more acidic byproducts — the cycle feeds itself
  5. Pressure builds until the gelcoat bulges (a visible blister) and eventually ruptures

Why resin choice matters here specifically

ResinCross-link chemistryHydrolysis resistance
PolyesterEster linkageWeakest
Vinyl esterEster linkage, tighter backbone slows water penetrationBetter, not immune
EpoxyEther linkageStrongest — this is why repairs use epoxy regardless of the hull's original resin

Repair timeline

StepWhat happensTime
1. Remove gelcoatGrind, sandblast, or peel down to laminateDays
2. Dry the hullMoisture meter must read below ~3% before proceedingDays to weeks — the bottleneck step, cannot be rushed
3. Fair & sealEpoxy resin + fairing compound over exposed laminateDays
4. RefinishRe-gelcoat or paint to match hullDays

The blister-warranty catch (Potts, via Soundings Online). A vinyl ester skin coat + blister warranty often comes bundled with a no-sanding restriction, enforced via a no-sanding primer under the bottom paint. When that primer fails, the owner must choose between peeling bottom paint or sanding — and sanding voids the warranty. Raise this proactively in a sales conversation rather than let a buyer discover it later.
