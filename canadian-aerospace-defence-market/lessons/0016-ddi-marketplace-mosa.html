DDI Marketplace, MOSA & Interoperability

A genuinely new kind of door — not a single program to chase, but a standing pre-qualification marketplace across all of them. The application window closes August 14, 2026.

This is time-sensitive, not background reading. The Defence Drone Initiative (DDI) launched July 23, 2026, and its Request for Supply Arrangement (RFSA) is open on CanadaBuys only until August 14, 2026. Everything else in this lesson is context for a decision that has a real, near-term deadline.

What DDI actually is

DND and the Defence Investment Agency launched the DDI to move away from procuring individual uncrewed-systems capabilities through lengthy one-off competitions. Instead, it establishes a standing marketplace of pre-qualified Canadian suppliers — startups, SMEs, established defence manufacturers, and Indigenous businesses — that the CAF can draw from rapidly as requirements emerge. Observers have described it as Canada's peacetime version of Ukraine's Brave1 platform.

Sources: DND, July 2026; Canadian Global Affairs Institute.

The five qualification streams

StreamCovers
1. Uncrewed & counter-uncrewed systems and componentsPlatforms and components — the most direct fit for a harness/electrical-assembly manufacturer
2. Data / electronic warfare / cyber systemsCommunications and data systems
3. Integration, interoperability & engineering servicesSystems engineering, MOSA and open architecture, prototyping, modification, interface development, safety cases, technical documentation, configuration management
4. Test and evaluation servicesTesting and training
5. Innovation & experimentation laneLow-barrier entry point for early-stage technology

For Kanata specifically, Stream 1 (components) is the natural fit given the actual manufacturing capability; Stream 3 is where the MOSA/interoperability requirements below actually get evaluated.

What MOSA means, and why it's on the application

A Modular Open Systems Approach (MOSA) is a design and acquisition strategy built around open standards and modular, loosely-coupled components that can be sourced from different, independent vendors rather than locked to one supplier's proprietary architecture. It exists to prevent vendor lock-in and let a buyer swap or upgrade individual modules without redesigning the whole system.

DDI's own Technology Questionnaire asks bidders directly: does the system use open interfaces and MOSA? Can Canada integrate third-party payloads, radios, sensors, batteries, and software into it? Do interface control documents (ICDs) exist? Can it operate without vendor cloud services? These questions are the real, operational meaning of "interoperability" and "interchangeability" on this application — not abstract principles, but specific yes/no facts about whether a supplier's product can plug into other suppliers' equipment using documented, non-proprietary interfaces.

For Kanata, this translates directly: can the harnesses and electrical assemblies you'd offer be described with standard, documented connector/interface specifications rather than one-off proprietary designs? Having (or being able to produce) an interface control document for a given assembly is a concrete, checkable answer to exactly what this questionnaire is asking.

Set expectations correctly

Passing the pass/fail screening (technical experience, operational capacity, security clearance) gets a company a Supply Arrangement — a place on a pre-qualified shortlist. It is explicitly not a contract, not money, and not a guaranteed opportunity. It's the same "engage the process, not a specific paycheque" logic Lesson 4 covered on CPSP's pre-contract window, just compressed into weeks instead of years.
