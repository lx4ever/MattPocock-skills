The CAF UxS Enterprise Model

From official CAF/DIA briefing materials: the strategic framework DDI (Lesson 16) and the broader UAS landscape (Lesson 17) actually sit inside — and it corrects a few things those lessons got narrower than they should have been.

Source note: this lesson is built from conference presentation slides photographed in person, not a published document with a URL — there's no link to cite the way every other lesson in this workspace does. Treat it as a real, primary-source briefing, but one that can't be re-verified by clicking through the way the rest of this workspace's sourcing can.

The five pillars

The CAF's UxS (uncrewed systems) strategy rests on five mutually reinforcing pillars — and the briefing is explicit that they have to move together: "Procurement modernization alone will fail if technical architecture and sovereign industrial capacity aren't spinning at the exact same speed."

PillarWhat it means
1. Accelerate acquisitionModernizing procurement pathways to match technology cycles — this is DDI (Lesson 16)
2. Standardize the digital backboneEnforcing open digital architecture and data interoperability — this is MOSA (below)
3. Build sovereign industrial capacityEnsuring domestic supply chain resilience and scale — this is the "sovereign Canadian provider" language seen throughout Lessons 15, 17, 18
4. Institutionalize test, training and sustainmentCommon professional standards and lifecycle support — DDI's Stream 4 and the CUAS Sandbox (Lesson 17)
5. Enable decentralized innovationEmpowering the operational edge with enterprise-level safety frameworks — DDI's Stream 5

Reading Lessons 16–18 against this framework: none of them were standalone programs. They're pieces of one five-pillar enterprise strategy, which is worth keeping in mind when reading any future UxS-related announcement — ask which pillar it belongs to.

Correction to Lesson 16: Stream 1 is broader than aerial drones

Lesson 16 described DDI's Stream 1 as "uncrewed and counter-uncrewed systems and components," which read as aerial-drone-focused. The official framing names it "Hardware" and lists it explicitly as UAS, UGV, USV, UUV, and attritable systems — uncrewed aerial and ground and surface and underwater vehicles. That's a materially wider door for Kanata than "drones" alone suggested: ground and maritime uncrewed vehicle harnessing is arguably a more direct extension of Kanata's existing naval/land experience than aerial platforms are.

The five streams have official names, restated here more precisely than Lesson 16 had them:

StreamNameCovers
1HardwareUAS, UGV, USV, UUV, attritable systems
2Nerve CenterC2, comms, data, EW & cyber — datalinks, mesh networks, SATCOM, edge computing
3Connective TissueIntegration, interoperability & engineering — MOSA interfaces, safety protocols, integration points
4ValidationTest, evaluation & training — test stands, simulation data, analysis workflows
5EvolutionInnovation & experimentation — rapid prototyping, innovation challenges, co-development frameworks

Correction to Lesson 16: what qualifying actually leads to

Lesson 16 correctly said a Supply Arrangement isn't a contract, but didn't explain the mechanism after qualification. The briefing calls it "funnel to flywheel": the RFSA is the funnel that pre-qualifies suppliers, and once qualified, they enter a flywheel of persistent, competitive mini-solicitations — ongoing, repeated small competitions among the pre-qualified pool, not one giant competition and not a guaranteed drip of work either. The explicit paradigm: "Qualification is the entry point, not the final competition." Getting qualified is necessary but not sufficient — it buys entry into a recurring competitive process, and reduces the administrative friction of that process, rather than buying a place in line.

Correction to Lesson 16: the real MOSA requirements, and a reassurance about IP

Lesson 16 gave a generic definition of MOSA. The briefing gives CAF's actual five requirements directly:

  • Separation of platform and mission systems.
  • Defined, controlled, and open/accessible interface standards.
  • Portable data and software.
  • Competitive component replacement without redesigning the entire system.
  • Configuration control and cyber assurance.

The briefing is explicit about a worry an SME might reasonably have here: "The objective is not 'open source everything.' It is accessible interfaces." and, as a stated key insight: "Industry retains valuable intellectual property. The mandate is disciplined interface control to prevent dependence on closed, inaccessible systems." MOSA compliance doesn't mean handing over proprietary harness or connector designs — it means documenting the interface boundary (the ICD, per Lesson 16) so components can be swapped, while everything behind that boundary stays Kanata's own IP.

The bigger shift this all sits inside

The briefing frames this as a wholesale change in acquisition philosophy, not just a new marketplace mechanism:

Traditional acquisitionFuture enterprise generation
Platform acquisitionContinuous capability generation
Fixed specificationsOperational outcomes and minimum thresholds
Long technology baselinesIterative technology refresh
Proprietary integrationModular open systems
Centralized experimentationInnovation at the operational edge
Limited production quantitiesScalable and surge-responsive capacity

Read against Lesson 14's Deloitte finding (primes are capacity-constrained, not demand-constrained), this table is the CAF-specific version of the same shift: away from one-off platform buys, toward a standing, scalable, modular supplier ecosystem it can draw on repeatedly. That's the environment DDI, MOSA, and the whole UxS Enterprise Model are built to produce.
