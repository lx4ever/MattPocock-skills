Lesson 14 · Canadian Aerospace, Space and Defence Market

The Global Backdrop: Why Primes Need Kanata Right Now

Every lesson so far explained why a Canadian prime is legally obligated to subcontract (the ITB Value Proposition, Lesson 1). Deloitte's 2026 Aerospace and Defense Industry Outlook gives a second, independent reason: primes are running out of capacity to do the work themselves.

The headline: money is not the constraint

Defense-tech venture capital hit a record US$19.8B in Q1 2026 alone — up 146% year over year. Spending priorities are shifting toward munitions, drones and counter-UAS, autonomy, missile defense, space, cyber, and industrial-base expansion. Demand across the sector is, in Deloitte's words, "structurally strong." That matches everything found across this workspace's 13 program-level lessons: real contracts, real dollars, real urgency.

The binding constraint: capacity, not demand

Deloitte is explicit that the industry is "increasingly won or lost on production capacity, certification throughput, and resilient supply chains," not on demand. Two things are named as the actual binding constraints: supply chain performance and workforce capacity. The workforce numbers are stark — production-critical roles are seeing attrition around 15%, more than double the cross-industry average (AIA/McKinsey, 2025), and PwC projects a retirement-driven gap of millions of workers, with 29% of the workforce over 55 as of 2023. Specific bottlenecks named in the midyear update: insufficient certified labor, test cells, tooling, repair approvals, parts availability, inspection capacity, and engine-module supply.

Sources: Deloitte, 2026 Aerospace and Defense Industry Outlook; Deloitte, Midyear Update.

The sentence that matters most for Kanata specifically

Fitch, cited in Deloitte's midyear update: "parts of the supply base remain fragile, particularly smaller sub-tier suppliers — making multi-year demand visibility and sustained commitments critical for stabilization." That's not abstract industry commentary — it's a direct description of Kanata's own position, and it names the exact medicine: multi-year visibility and sustained commitments. That's precisely what an ITB Value Proposition obligation is — a contractually locked-in, multi-year Canadian-content commitment. Everything this workspace has been chasing since Lesson 1 turns out to be the specific mechanism the wider industry says fragile sub-tier suppliers need.

Two reasons a prime subcontracts, not one

This adds a second rationale on top of Lesson 1's ITB mechanism, and the two reinforce each other:

  • Legal: the prime is contractually obligated to deliver Canadian-content value under its Value Proposition (Lesson 1).
  • Operational: the prime may genuinely not have the in-house capacity — certified labor, test cells, tooling, inspection throughput — to do all the work itself, regardless of the obligation.

A pitch built only on the first reason reads as "help me hit a compliance number." A pitch that leads with the second reads as "I can solve a capacity problem you actually have." Both are true simultaneously on most of the programs covered in this workspace — worth using both, not just the ITB angle Lessons 2–13 have leaned on so far.

What this changes about outreach

Concretely: worth adding a line to outreach drafts (like the ones drafted for Provencher, Hurst, and Hey) that speaks to reliable, multi-year production capacity — not just certifications and Value Proposition fit. A prime reading "we can commit sustained capacity over the life of this program" is answering Deloitte's stabilization criterion directly, in the primes' own current language, not just Kanata's.

Primary source to read next Deloitte's Midyear Update — the more current of the two reports, and the one with the sub-tier fragility finding this lesson is built on.
