Setting Up a Company for a Sino Polymer Partnership

Federal incorporation, multiple shareholders, import/distribution of resin into Ontario — the company-formation and financial-structure questions sitting underneath the entire sales mission this workspace has been building toward.

The one-sentence version: a CBCA corporation (not a legal partnership) with multiple shareholders, structured with separate share classes and a real shareholders' agreement from day one — and this is general structural information, not legal, tax, or accounting advice. Engage a corporate lawyer and a CPA before finalizing anything.

1. Company type: a CBCA corporation, not a legal partnership

Worth being precise about terminology, since it has real consequences: with multiple owners, the goal is shareholders in a corporation, not partners in a legal partnership. A general partnership exposes each partner to unlimited personal liability for the business's debts and each other's actions — a corporation is a separate legal person, and shareholders' liability is generally limited to what they invested. Given the business involves importing product and taking on product-liability exposure (resin becomes part of a structural pipe liner), the liability shield matters.

Federal incorporation (CBCA) fits a Sino Polymer distribution/partnership goal well: nationwide name protection, and the right to carry on business in any province. It still requires extra-provincial registration in Ontario (and any other province of operation) — federal incorporation doesn't exempt a company from that. Standard entity type: a numbered company (e.g., "1234567 Canada Inc.") or a named corporation ("[Name] Inc./Ltd./Corp.").

2. Company name endings: Ltd., Inc., Corp. — and why "Co." is different

This trips a lot of people up, especially coming from US business terminology where these really are different structures. In Canada, they mostly aren't.

Ltd., Inc., Corp. — same thing, different label. Under the CBCA (and Ontario's corporate statute), a corporation must end its name with one of six legal elements, in English/French pairs: Limited/Ltd. (Limitée/Ltée), Incorporated/Inc. (Incorporée/Inc.), and Corporation/Corp. (Corporation/Corp.). These are functionally identical. Picking one over another has zero effect on liability protection, taxation, governance, or anything substantive — it's a pure style choice at incorporation. A company doesn't become "more of a corporation" by using one over another.
"Co." is the odd one out — it's not actually a legal element. "Co." by itself doesn't grant corporate/limited-liability status in Canada; it isn't one of the six recognized legal elements above. It can mean one of two things instead: part of an unincorporated business name — a sole proprietorship or general partnership trading as "Smith & Co." carries no liability shield at all, despite sounding corporate — or a word inside a corporation's name that still needs a real legal element attached, e.g. "ABC Trading Co. Ltd." is valid because "Ltd." is doing the actual legal work; "Co." alone is just part of the brand name. Seeing "Co." on a business name tells you nothing about whether it's actually incorporated — worth checking the actual ending (or a corporate registry search) rather than assuming.

If the comparison being drawn is actually entity types rather than name suffixes, that's a different axis entirely:

One common source of confusion worth flagging directly: the US "LLC" doesn't have a direct Canadian equivalent. A handful of provinces (Nova Scotia, Alberta, British Columbia) allow "Unlimited Liability Companies" (ULC) — but that's the opposite of what it sounds like. It's a niche structure mainly used for US cross-border tax planning, not a general small-business option, and it actually removes the liability shield rather than adding the flexibility a US LLC provides.

3. The incorporation steps

  1. NUANS name search (if using a real name, not a numbered company) — reserves the name and checks for conflicts.
  2. File Articles of Incorporation with Corporations Canada (online via their federal incorporation portal, or through a lawyer/incorporation service). This sets the authorized share structure, registered office, and initial directors.
  3. Get the Certificate of Incorporation.
  4. Register extra-provincially in Ontario via ServiceOntario.
  5. Get a CRA Business Number (BN), then register the specific program accounts needed:
    • RC — corporate income tax
    • RT — GST/HST (mandatory once revenue exceeds $30K; worth registering from day one anyway to recover import GST as an input tax credit)
    • RM — import/export account (needed for bringing in containers from Sino Polymer)
    • RP — payroll, if/when paying salaries
  6. Open a separate corporate bank account — never commingle personal and corporate funds; CRA and creditors both look at this.
  7. Corporate minute book: bylaws, initial resolutions, share issuance/share certificates, director/officer appointments.

4. Multi-owner structure

Two things matter more here than for a solo shareholder:

Share classes. A common setup for multiple founders is separate classes of shares (e.g., Class A for one shareholder, Class B for another) rather than everyone holding identical common shares. This lets the corporation pay discretionary dividends — different amounts, different timing, to different shareholders — instead of being locked into paying every shareholder the same dividend per share. Worth setting up at incorporation, since restructuring share classes later is more expensive and can trigger tax consequences.
A shareholders' agreement, drafted by a corporate lawyer, covering: ownership percentages, decision-making/voting rights, what happens if a shareholder wants out (buy-sell terms, valuation method), deadlock resolution, non-compete/non-solicit, and what happens on death or disability. This is the single most commonly regretted omission in multi-owner startups — get it done before money starts moving, not after a disagreement.

  • If the corporation qualifies as a Canadian-Controlled Private Corporation (CCPC), active business income up to $500,000 gets the small business deduction — combined federal+Ontario rate around 12%, versus roughly 26.5% general rate above that threshold. This changes the salary/dividend math meaningfully and is worth confirming the corporation qualifies (ownership/control tests apply).
  • With separate share classes, each shareholder's salary-vs-dividend mix can be decided independently — one shareholder might want salary for RRSP room and mortgage-qualification income, another might prefer dividends.
  • Dividends must respect the rights attached to each share class — exactly why the class structure in Section 4 matters.
  • A common real-world pattern is a modest "reasonable" salary (enough for RRSP room and CPP credit) topped up with dividends, but the actual optimal split depends on each shareholder's total household income and other tax situation — this needs a real projection from an accountant, not a rule of thumb.

6. Financial structure, specific to an import/distribution business

Since the company's actual business is importing Sino Polymer resin (Lesson 60's 20FCL/IBC/drum logistics) and reselling to Ontario CIPP contractors, a few structural pieces go beyond generic incorporation:

Landed cost, not FOB price, is the real cost base. FOB/CIF price from Sino Polymer + ocean freight + customs duties (confirm the correct HS tariff code — Canada has no free trade agreement with China, so MFN duty rates apply) + customs brokerage + GST on import (5%, recoverable as an input tax credit if GST-registered) + inland trucking to the warehouse. Margin quoted off the factory price alone will be wrong.
  • Working capital gap. Import businesses typically pay the supplier (or at least a deposit) well before customers pay them — factor in a line of credit or trade financing, especially while inventory sits in a warehouse per Lesson 59's shelf-life constraints.
  • Inventory risk is a real financial line item, not just an operational one — the shelf-life/temperature findings from Lessons 59–60 translate directly into inventory carrying cost and write-off risk if resin sits too long or gets mishandled.
  • Shareholder loan accounts — if shareholders advance funds to the company or draw funds beyond salary/declared dividends, CRA tracks this closely (a shareholder loan not repaid or properly documented within the required period can be taxed as income). Keep this clean from day one.
  • Insurance: general liability, product liability (a real exposure given resin ends up as part of a structural liner), and marine cargo insurance for the shipments themselves.
  • A Holdco/Opco split is a common structure worth considering once the business has real retained earnings or is taking on liability risk — an operating company (Opco) runs the import/resale business and takes on the liability exposure, while a holding company (Holdco) owns the Opco's shares and can receive tax-free intercorporate dividends, keeping accumulated profit and any major assets shielded from Opco's operating risk. Not necessary at the very start, but worth planning for if the business scales.

The honest caveat

This is general structural information, not legal, tax, or accounting advice — and getting the details wrong here (share class terms, CCPC qualification, shareholder loan treatment, import duty classification) has real financial and legal consequences. Before finalizing anything, engage a corporate lawyer for the incorporation and shareholders' agreement, and a CPA for the actual salary/dividend split and bookkeeping setup — ideally both before filing, not after.
