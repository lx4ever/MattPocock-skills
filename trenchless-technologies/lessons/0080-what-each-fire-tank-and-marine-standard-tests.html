Lesson 80: What Each Fire, Tank and Marine Standard Actually Tests

Seven names from one certification slide — UL 1316, UL 1746, UL 1856, UL 94, ASTM E84, EN 45545-2 and Lloyd’s Register — sorted into three families by the question each one answers.

Lesson 78 taught you to ask which grade, which standard, which scope. This lesson fills in the second question: what each standard on that slide actually tests, and on what object. The win: when a prospect or a slide says “UL listed” or “Class A,” you can say in one sentence what it does and doesn’t tell you.

Two names corrected before we start. The request listed “UL 1748” and “ASTM E86.” I found no UL 1748 in any source read. The workspace’s own Sino Polymer documents (Lesson 78) name UL 1746, so that is what is covered here. For “E86,” the Class A flame-spread test on that slide is ASTM E84, also from Lesson 78. If either correction is wrong, say so and I’ll fix the lesson.

Three families, three different questions

FamilyStandardsThe question it answers
Fuel-tankUL 1316, UL 1746, UL 1856Will this tank, coating or lining system contain petroleum fuels without leaking, buckling or corroding underground?
FireUL 94, ASTM E84, EN 45545-2How does this material burn: self-extinguishing, surface flame spread, or heat, smoke and toxicity in a train?
MarineLloyd’s RegisterHas an independent classification society approved this product and its production for ship use?

None of the three questions is “does this resin perform as a sewer liner?” — which is why Lesson 78 found none of them in the Toronto or Peel tenders.

The fuel-tank trio

These are the standards that sit around buried fuel tanks, and they cover three different jobs:

  • UL 1316 covers fibre-reinforced plastic tanks — single or double wall — for underground storage of flammable and combustible liquids. Its stated concerns include leakage, buckling under external pressure, internal test pressure, and whether the materials are compatible with the stored fluid and the ground soil. The tanks are built, inspected and leak-tested at the factory as complete vessels (scope as listed by ANSI). A trade summary describes 16 tests, including air-oven aging and immersion in liquids representing the actual product (Mobility Plaza overview).
  • UL 1746 covers external corrosion protection systems for steel tanks — cathodic protection, FRP composite systems, and jacketed systems — applied at the factory (scope as listed by GlobalSpec). The same overview describes a durability test with samples immersed for 270 days that must not crack, flake or expose the base material.
  • UL 1856 covers nonmetallic retrofit systems installed inside existing steel or fibreglass underground fuel tanks. It defines three types: lining (containment only), upgrade (primary plus secondary containment, shares load with the old tank) and structural (stands on its own). It explicitly excludes installer safety, external corrosion protection, and thin coatings applied directly to the tank surface (UL Standards store listing).
Worth noticing: UL 1856 is the only one of the six standards that describes a liner going inside an existing host, which is the same basic idea as the CIPP in your mission. But it is a fuel-tank retrofit standard with its own tests and fluids; it says nothing about a sewer liner.

What I can and can’t say about Sino Polymer. Lesson 78 records UL 1316 / UL 1746 against MERICAN 9505 (double-wall fuel tanks) and notes CAMX also listed UL 1856. These standards test finished tanks, coatings or retrofit systems, not a resin on its own. So a resin supplier can honestly claim “used in a UL-listed tank” or “tested for these fluids,” but those are different claims. That reading is an inference from the scopes above, not something the Sino Polymer documents say. The question to ask is: listed to what, for whom, as which product?

The fire trio: three scales you can’t compare

StandardWhat is burnedHow it’s scored
UL 94 V-0Small plastic specimen, typically 127 × 12.7 mm, held vertically (UL 94 overview)Burning stops within 10 seconds, and no flaming drips ignite the cotton below. V-1 and V-2 allow up to 30 seconds. Thickness matters.
ASTM E84 Class AA 24-inch-wide, 24-foot-long sample in the Steiner tunnel, 10 minutes (Intertek summary)Flame Spread Index 0–25 and Smoke Developed Index up to 450. Class B is 26–75, Class C 76–200.
EN 45545-2Materials and components for railway vehicles, by position (interior, exterior, furniture, electrical and so on)A hazard level HL1 to HL3 (set by how hard the vehicle is to evacuate) and a requirement set (R1 to R26 or so) that picks the tests: cone calorimeter (ISO 5660-1), smoke density and toxicity (ISO 5659-2 with EN 17084), flame spread (ISO 5658-2) (Giordano overview).

Different specimens, different flames, different pass marks. A V-0 plastic strip tells you nothing about tunnel flame spread, and Class A tells you nothing about rail smoke toxicity. That is why Lesson 78’s table keeps each one attached to specific grades: MFE 707H/707A (brominated vinyl esters) for E84, the flame-retardant epoxies for UL 94 V-0, and named grades at HL2 or HL3 for EN 45545-2.

One more thing the fire results depend on: Lesson 78 recorded that 707A’s Class A claim carries a note, “with 5% Sb₂O₃ added.” A fire result belongs to a formulation, not to the product name alone.

UL 94 and thickness: the catch inside the rating

A UL 94 classification is tied to the thickness that was tested. The same compound can pass V-0 at one thickness and only V-1 or V-2 at another (Passive Components; SpecialChem). Test bars are about 125 × 13 mm; common thicknesses are 0.7, 1.5 and 3.0 mm, though the test can run at any thickness up to 13 mm. UL’s own listing (the “Yellow Card”) shows each tested thickness, and a datasheet rating only counts at the thickness it states.

Tested thicknessResult
3.0 mmV-0
1.5 mmV-0
0.8 mmV-1

Illustrative numbers only — not from any real datasheet. This compound can claim V-0 at 1.5 mm. A 1.0 mm wall falls in the gap between the 1.5 mm V-0 result and the 0.8 mm V-1 result, so V-0 can’t be claimed for it. A thinner section may burn longer or drip flaming particles, which is what V-0 forbids. (That last point is general reasoning, not stated in the sources.)

The sales question: Lesson 78 records “UL 94 V-0” for 3313-3, 3314, 3315 and 3316, and the notes show no thickness with it. Before quoting it, ask: at what thickness was this grade rated, and is that at or below the customer’s part?

The marine one: Lloyd’s Register

Lloyd’s Register runs Type Approval: an independent check that a product design and its manufacturing process consistently conform to recognised standards, conventions or LR’s own Rules, including production controls and traceability, with sample testing (LR coating certification page; LR test specifications). That is a different kind of claim from a one-off test result: it covers the production system as well.

What I could not confirm: I did not find a public LR page describing a resin- or gelcoat-specific approval scheme, and the LR piping chapter I opened covers metallic piping. So what scheme sits behind “Lloyd’s Register” for MFE 711, MERICAN 9406/9709 and the gelcoats is still open. Lesson 78 already says what’s needed: a current certificate naming the exact product and scope.

One question for any of these

  1. What was tested? A tank, a coating, a plastic strip, a building panel, a train component, or a production process.
  2. Is it this grade, in this formulation? Ask for the certificate or listing, not the logo.
  3. Is it a sewer question? If the answer is fuel tanks, rail or ships, the claim is real but for another market.

Quick check

What Each Standard Tests

1. What does a UL 94 V-0 rating describe?

2. What does UL 1856 cover?

3. A resin’s product earned ASTM E84 Class A. Does that show EN 45545-2 HL3?

4. What sets the hazard level (HL1 to HL3) in EN 45545-2?

5. A slide lists “Lloyd’s Register” beside MFE 711. What is still needed?

6. A grade passed UL 94 V-0 at 1.5 mm and V-1 at 0.8 mm. Can a 1.0 mm wall claim V-0?
