Lesson 41: From a Resin TDS to a Liner Design

Lessons 10–11 gave you a real resin TDS. Lesson 39 gave you the design process. This is the missing link between them — which TDS numbers actually plug in, and which parts of the design a TDS can never answer on its own.

The one-sentence version: a resin TDS gets you exactly one input, out of roughly six the design actually needs — and the gap between "what's on the TDS" and "what the design requires" is precisely where most of the qualification work in Lessons 26–27's certification checklist lives.

What's actually on a typical resin TDS

Using the Sino Polymer brochure from Lessons 10–11 as the concrete reference point, a real CIPP resin TDS typically gives you:

  • Initial flexural modulus (ASTM D790) — ~2,900–4,100 MPa across Sino Polymer's line
  • Initial flexural strength (ASTM D790) — ~100–170 MPa
  • Tensile strength (ASTM D638) — ~60–160 MPa
  • Heat deflection temperature (ASTM D648)
  • Non-volatile content (Lesson 20) — cure-completeness and shrinkage indicator
  • Sometimes: viscosity, gel time range, qualitative resistance claims ("excellent corrosion resistance")

That's a real, useful data set — but notice what's already missing before you even get to the design math: no density figure in most brochures, no long-term (creep) data, and every number on the sheet is neat resin, not the cured composite that actually goes in the ground.

Mapping each TDS field to where it plugs in — or doesn't

TDS fieldWhere it plugs into the design
Initial flexural modulus/strengthNot usable directly. Has to be converted to a long-term value via a creep retention factor (Lesson 39) before it can go into the buckling equation. The TDS gives you the starting number for that conversion, not the finished input.
Non-volatile contentFeeds Lesson 40's shrinkage/loss allowance in the resin quantity takeoff, and is a proxy for cure quality generally (Lesson 20).
DensityDirectly used in Lesson 40's final volume-to-mass conversion. If it's not on the TDS — and it often isn't — this is a real, simple gap to close by just asking the supplier, before any quantity takeoff can be trusted.
Tensile strengthNot used in the gravity-pipe buckling check at all. It matters for forcemain/watermain (pressure pipe) design instead — Lessons 24–25's pressure-pipe additions to OPSS.MUNI 444/445.
Poisson's ratioAlmost never printed on a TDS. Design work defaults to a generic assumption (≈0.3) for CIPP resin systems generically, absent product-specific test data — worth asking for directly if precision matters.
Everything else on the sheetUseful sales/qualification context (Lessons 10–11), but doesn't feed the thickness calculation directly.

The gap that breaks a TDS-only design attempt: neat resin vs. composite

This is the single point where a TDS-based design goes wrong if it's treated as sufficient on its own. Every number above is measured on cast, cured resin alone — no felt, no fiberglass. The F1216 buckling equation needs the properties of the actual composite (resin plus reinforcement, at the specific fiber-to-resin ratio a real liner uses), which behaves differently — usually stiffer, because the reinforcement carries load the neat resin alone doesn't. The certification checklist reference doc already flagged this precisely: Sino Polymer's neat-resin numbers clear Peel's 2,400 MPa non-reinforced bar comfortably, but say essentially nothing about whether a composite using that resin clears the 8,000 MPa reinforced bar — that's a composite-system number a TDS cannot supply, no matter how good the resin is.

The practical six-step workflow

  1. Pull the initial modulus/strength from the TDS. This is your starting point, not your design input.
  2. Get or estimate the long-term (creep-corrected) value. The real path is ASTM D2990 testing of the actual composite (10,000+ hours, per the certification checklist) — the TDS alone never has this. Where that testing doesn't exist yet, a conservative assumed creep retention factor can be used for early estimating, but it has to be labeled as an assumption, not treated as a substitute for real data in an actual bid.
  3. Get composite-level data at the real fiber/resin ratio. This is usually the contractor's or liner manufacturer's responsibility, not the resin supplier's alone (Lesson 26) — the resin TDS is an input to that composite testing, not a replacement for it.
  4. Plug the long-term composite values into the site-specific F1216 calculation alongside the groundwater, soil, live load, ovality, K-factor, and safety factor inputs from Lesson 39.
  5. Round the resulting thickness up to whatever billing increment the actual contract uses (1.5mm units on a Peel job, per Lesson 28).
  6. Run the resin quantity takeoff (Lesson 40) using that final, rounded thickness — plus the TDS's non-volatile content and a confirmed density figure, not an assumed one.

A worked illustration of exactly where the TDS stops being enough

Take Sino Polymer's ~2,900–4,100 MPa initial flexural modulus range. That number alone gets you through Step 1. Everything after it — the creep retention factor, the composite (not neat-resin) modulus, the site-specific loads, the final thickness, the resin volume — depends on data the TDS doesn't contain. A pitch or an estimate built by stopping at Step 1 and treating the initial neat-resin number as if it were the design input is exactly the mistake the certification checklist was written to catch.

What to actually request from a supplier when the TDS falls short

This mirrors the certification checklist's open-items list directly, reframed around the design workflow specifically:

  • Resin density, if not already on the TDS — needed for Step 6, and often simply missing rather than hard to get.
  • ASTM D2990 long-term creep data, ideally on the composite, not just the neat resin — needed for Step 2.
  • Composite (not neat-resin) flexural test data at a realistic job fiber ratio — needed for Step 3, and the single highest-value document a supplier can produce to actually move a design forward.
  • Confirmation of Poisson's ratio if the design is sensitive enough to need better than the generic 0.3 assumption.
