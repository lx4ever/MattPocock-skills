Lesson 1: The Trenchless Families

Sort SIPP, CIPP, fold-and-form, and UV-cured lining into the right buckets — before you're in front of a client.

Every trenchless rehab method answers the same question: how do you build a new pipe wall inside an old, failing one, without digging a trench? The four terms you're starting with sound like four peers. They aren't. One of them is a curing detail, not a family of its own — and mixing that up in front of a client is the kind of slip that costs credibility.

The shared shape

Every method below does three things in some order: gets into the pipe through an existing access point (usually a manhole), becomes the new pipe wall, and finishes as a seamless liner bonded or fitted tightly to the old one. What differs is what goes in and how it becomes solid.

Three real families, one imposter

MethodWhat goes inHow it becomes the new wallTell
CIPP
Cured-in-place pipe		 A flexible tube (felt or fiberglass) soaked in resin Pulled or inverted into place, then cured hard — by ambient air, hot water, steam, or UV/LED light tube + resin + cure
SIPP
Spray-in-place pipe		 Liquid polymer (epoxy, polyurethane, polyurea) or geopolymer mortar — no tube at all Sprayed or spin-cast directly onto the cleaned pipe wall by a robotic head; bonds by adhesion spray, no tube
Fold-and-form A real solid thermoplastic pipe (PE or PVC), factory-deformed into a folded H, U, or C cross-section Winched in folded, then reformed round using heat and pressure (steam + compressed air) an actual pipe, reshaped twice
The catch: UV-cured lining is not a fourth family — it's one of CIPP's curing options. Same resin-soaked tube (usually fiberglass), same insertion, but instead of pumping hot water or steam through it for hours, a train of UV lights is pulled through the tube to cure it in minutes. It's still CIPP. Calling it a separate method next to SIPP and fold-and-form is the exact mix-up worth avoiding.

Why the cure method matters anyway

Even though UV isn't a separate family, which cure method a CIPP job uses is a real, client-relevant decision:

  • Hot water / steam cure: slower (often hours), needs large volumes of water or steam on site, that water picks up resin residue and needs proper disposal.
  • UV cure: much faster (often minutes per run), no water involved, and the fiberglass-based liners it uses tend to be stiffer — useful when the host pipe is in bad shape and needs more structural help from the liner itself.

Quick check: which family?

1. A robotic arm sprays epoxy directly onto the inside of a corroded storm pipe. No tube is inserted.

2. A coiled PVC pipe, folded into a U-shape, is winched into a sewer line, then inflated back to round with steam and air pressure.

3. A fiberglass tube soaked in light-sensitive resin is pulled into place, then a train of UV lamps is towed through it to harden it in minutes.
