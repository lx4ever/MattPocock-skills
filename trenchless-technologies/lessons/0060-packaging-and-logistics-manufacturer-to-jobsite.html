Lesson 60: Packaging and Logistics — Manufacturer to Job Site

Real packaging specs from Sino Polymer's own catalogue page — three container formats, real net/gross weights, and a genuine container-capacity gap between them that nothing in this workspace had surfaced before.

Every prior lesson on Sino Polymer's product line has been about what's in the resin. This one is about the box it ships in — a real, primary-source packaging table that turns out to have a genuinely useful freight-efficiency finding buried in it, plus an open question this workspace can't answer yet: what happens to shelf life during the weeks a drum or IBC actually spends in transit.

Worth being precise about what "resins" means here: the IBC and drum formats are described as being for resins generally — the tube-impregnation resin this workspace has been tracking since Lesson 10. The 20L pail format is specifically called out for gel coat, a related but different product (a surface-finish/barrier coat, not the structural CIPP tube resin itself) — worth not conflating the two when a packaging question comes up for an actual resin order.

The real finding: bulk IBC packaging fits meaningfully more resin per container than steel drums

A full 20FCL of 1000L IBCs carries 20,000 kg of net resin. The same container loaded with 200L steel drums carries 16,000 kg net — 25% less, in the same box. The gap comes from steel drums' proportionally heavier packaging overhead (10% vs. the IBC's 5.6%) plus the pallet framework needed to stack and secure them, both eating into the container's usable payload. For a resin order large enough to matter, that's a real, quantifiable freight-cost difference — more usable resin moving per container, and per dollar of ocean freight, in IBC format than in drums.

Steel drums still have a real, practical advantage the tonnage math doesn't capture: they split a large order into 200 kg increments instead of one 1000 kg bulk unit, which matters for a contractor who wants to draw down inventory in smaller batches, store partial pallets at a yard rather than a single large tank, or match a purchase quantity more closely to a specific job's takeoff (Lesson 40) without opening a partially-used IBC.

Sizing this against a real resin order

Lesson 40 worked a real illustrative takeoff: roughly 265 kg of resin for one 33-metre, 250mm-diameter segment at 9mm design thickness. Against these packaging formats, that's a little over one steel drum (200 kg net), or roughly a quarter of a single 1000L IBC — a concrete way to picture how a job-level resin quantity actually maps onto what shows up on a truck.

An open question this packaging data raises, that nothing in this workspace has answered yet

Lesson 59 established that heat is the real enemy of resin shelf life — it accelerates the same reaction the inhibitor is suppressing. Neither this packaging page nor any of the five SDSs reviewed in Lesson 58 states whether Sino Polymer's ocean freight from Shanghai runs in a temperature-controlled (reefer) container or a standard dry container. Standard ocean transit from China to a North American port typically runs several weeks, and a standard dry shipping container sitting on a ship or a port yard can reach meaningfully higher internal temperatures than the "cool, dry, well-ventilated" storage every SDS specifies. If shipping isn't temperature-controlled, a real, unanswered question follows directly from this workspace's own chemistry: how much of a drum or IBC's nominal shelf life gets consumed in transit, before it even reaches a contractor's yard? This is a genuinely concrete, specific question worth putting to Sino Polymer directly — not a hypothetical one, since the packaging and shelf-life data needed to ask it precisely now both exist in this workspace.

A practical road-transport constraint worth flagging, not asserting as fact

A full 20-IBC load's cargo weight (21,120 kg gross) sits right around a commonly cited road-transport payload limit for a 20-foot container (roughly 21,600 kg, though ISO/maritime limits run meaningfully higher, up to roughly 28,000 kg, and the real number depends on the specific carrier and the road-weight regulations of the receiving jurisdiction). The steel-drum load (17,600 kg gross) sits well clear of that same figure. This doesn't mean a full IBC container can't legally move by road in Ontario — that depends on rules this workspace hasn't checked directly — but it's a real, worth-confirming practical question before assuming "20 IBCs, no problem" translates cleanly from ocean freight to a truck able to deliver the full container inland.
