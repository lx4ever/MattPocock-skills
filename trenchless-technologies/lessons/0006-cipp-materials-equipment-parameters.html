Lesson 6: CIPP — Materials, Equipment, Parameters

What's actually in the tube, what runs the job site, and the numbers that make a proposal credible or suspect.

You know the six install steps. This lesson is one level down: the specific materials and equipment behind those steps, and the parameters that let you tell whether a contractor's spec sheet actually hangs together — or contradicts itself.

Materials

The tube

  • Felt — needle-punched polyester felt. The traditional choice; works with any cure method except UV (it's opaque and absorbent, so UV light can't cure through it evenly).
  • Fiberglass — required for UV cure, since light needs to pass through the tube wall to reach the resin. Also tends to be stiffer once cured than a felt-and-resin composite.

The resin

  • Polyester — the default for standard sanitary or storm mains: usually the most economical option that still clears ASTM requirements.
  • Vinyl ester — chemically closer to epoxy, but cures like polyester (free-radical, with styrene or another reactive diluent). Better chemical resistance than polyester, at a higher price.
  • Epoxy — properties vary widely by specific system, from stiff and brittle to very flexible. Often styrene-free, which matters for odor and emissions control — relevant for potable water lines or sensitive sites.
  • UV-cure systems — a light-sensitive resin (typically vinyl ester or acrylate-based, with a photoinitiator) paired with a fiberglass tube. Felt + UV is not a real combination.
Sales-relevant point: tube and resin aren't independent choices — they're paired with the cure method. A proposal specifying a felt tube with UV curing is internally inconsistent; UV needs the fiberglass tube to work at all. That's exactly the kind of thing worth catching before a bid goes to a client.

Equipment

  1. Wet-out / impregnation station. A mobile unit that meters and mixes the resin, vacuum-impregnates it into the tube, then runs it through nip/calibration rollers to spread it evenly and squeeze out trapped air.
  2. Refrigerated truck or trailer. Once wet-out, the tube is a perishable product — kept chilled in transit so the resin doesn't start curing before it's in the pipe.
  3. Insertion equipment. An inversion drum or tower (inversion) or a winch and cable (pull-in-place) — matches the method from Lesson 2.
  4. Cure equipment. A boiler (hot water), steam generator (steam), or a towed UV light train — whichever cure method was chosen.

Parameters worth knowing

ParameterTypical figure
Inversion / calibration pressureUp to roughly 30 psi, depending on diameter and tube thickness
Steam cure temperature & time~225–240°F, held 1–4 hours depending on length, thickness, and conditions
Cool-down before cuttingAt least 15 minutes, or until the far-end temperature reaches ~130°F, whichever is longer
UV light train speedRoughly 1 ft/minute
Tube thicknessEngineered per job to the pipe's structural requirements — not a fixed default

One more thing worth knowing exists, even without doing the math yourself: every CIPP job should have a wet-out report — documentation that the resin fully saturated the tube, with no dry spots. A dry spot is a weak point in the finished liner. You don't need to calculate saturation ratios to know to ask "do you have the wet-out report?"

Quick check

Spot the mismatch

1. A proposal specifies: felt tube, cured with a UV light train. What's wrong?

2. A proposal for a potable water line specifies a resin system, and the client is sensitive to on-site odor and emissions. Which resin is the strongest fit to flag as worth asking about?

3. A field report says the liner was inverted, held at 30 psi, then hot water was circulated at 230°F for two hours before a 20-minute cool-down. What's missing that you'd want to confirm exists somewhere in the paperwork?
