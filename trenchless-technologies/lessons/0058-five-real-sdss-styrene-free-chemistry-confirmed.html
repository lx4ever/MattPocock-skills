Lesson 58: Five Real SDSs — The Styrene-Free Chemistry, Confirmed

Five real, primary-source EU Safety Data Sheets from Sino Polymer, spanning epoxy, polyester, and both a styrene and a styrene-free vinyl ester — enough to finally answer, with real numbers, what "styrene-free" actually swaps in, and what that swap actually buys.

This workspace has cited flash point and styrene content as hazmat/odor proxies since Lesson 17, and built out the styrene-free trigger as a real contractual mechanism in Lesson 54. What's been missing is a set of real per-product SDS numbers spanning both sides of that trigger from the same supplier, on the same 16-section EU format. These five documents supply exactly that.

A pattern worth stating plainly: flash point and transport classification track styrene content almost exactly, not resin chemistry family. The two styrene-containing products — a polyester and a vinyl ester, otherwise different chemistries — land on the identical 39°C flash point and the identical UN1866 Class 3 PG III shipping classification, down to the identical hazard-statement set (H226/H315/H317/H319/H361d/H372). The styrene-free vinyl ester, built on largely the same base resin (below), clears both the flash point and the dangerous-goods classification entirely. Whether a product ships as regulated Class 3 dangerous goods appears to be a styrene question, not a "which resin family" question.

This is the first time both sides of Lesson 46's flash-point math have real product data

Lesson 46 worked out the exact regulatory thresholds a >100°C flash point clears — both the US 49 CFR §173.120 flammable-liquid (≤60°C) and combustible-liquid (60–93°C) brackets, and Canada's TDG Class 3 threshold — using a single data point, MFE 701-01T's >100°C claim from a marketing brochure. MFE 7516's SDS-stated ≥110°C flash point is a second, independently sourced confirmation of that same result, from a primary regulatory document rather than a brochure line. And now there's a real number on the other side of the comparison too: 39°C for both styrene-containing products, squarely inside Class 3 with room to spare (nowhere close to even the 60°C boundary). The gap between the two sides of the styrene-free trigger isn't marginal — it's the difference between a regulated dangerous-goods shipment and a completely unregulated one, for transport purposes.

What actually replaces styrene: a real answer, not a category

HPMA — methacrylic acid, monoester with propane-1,2-diol (CAS 27813-02-1) — is MFE 7516's reactive diluent, at 30–60% weight, standing in for styrene's usual 42–48% weight role in MFE 711. Every prior lesson treating "styrene-free" as a formulation category (11, 17, 46, 47, 54) has never had a named substance to point to for what's actually doing styrene's job — keeping the resin at a workable viscosity and providing the free-radical cure crosslinks. This SDS supplies that answer directly, with its own real toxicology and ecotoxicology data (Daphnia magna EC50 >143 mg/L, algae EC50 >97.2 mg/L) rather than a placeholder.

The same base resin, three different products

CAS 36425-16-8 — "4,4'-isopropylidenediphenol, oligomeric reaction products with 1-chloro-2,3-epoxypropane, reaction products with maleic anhydride and methacrylic acid," labelled "Epoxy acrylate" in two SDSs and "METHYL ACRYLIC ACID EPOXY ESTER" in the third — appears as a major component in all three resin products in this batch:

This is a genuinely new finding, not something Lessons 11 or 44's brochure-level catalog review could have shown. The same underlying epoxy-vinyl-ester base chemistry gets sold three ways depending on what it's paired with: alone in an epoxy resin system cured by a separate anhydride hardener, blended with styrene for a conventional vinyl ester, or blended with HPMA for a styrene-free vinyl ester. "Epoxy vs. vinyl ester" (Lesson 47) and "styrene vs. styrene-free" (Lesson 54) aren't actually two independent axes for this product family — they share a common base resin, differentiated by what's mixed in and what cures it.

MERICAN 3222 B: the epoxy cure chemistry gap Lesson 47 left open, filled with a real example

Lesson 47 described epoxy as curing "via an amine or anhydride hardener" without a concrete example of either. MERICAN 3222 B is 100% tetrahydromethylphthalic anhydride (CAS 11070-44-3) — a real, named anhydride-cure hardener, distinct from the amine-cure chemistry more commonly discussed in composites literature. Worth flagging: the hardener carries a meaningfully worse hazard profile than the resin side it pairs with — H318 (serious eye damage, more severe than 3222 A's H319 serious eye irritation) and H334 (respiratory sensitizer — may cause allergy or asthma symptoms if inhaled), on top of the same H317 skin-sensitization both sides share. That's a real PPE and handling-training distinction between the two drums of a two-component epoxy system, not something a single-line "epoxy resin" pitch would surface.

A genuinely counterintuitive transport-classification detail: despite carrying the more severe Danger signal word and H318/H334, MERICAN 3222 B ships as not dangerous goods under ADR/RID, IMDG, and IATA — while MERICAN 3222 A, with the milder Warning signal word, ships as UN3082 Class 9 (environmentally hazardous, on account of its H411 aquatic toxicity). GHS workplace hazard classification and UN transport classification are answering different questions — a resin's shipping class can't be inferred from its SDS signal word alone.

MERICAN 9406: a second, chemically distinct UV-curable CIPP product

MERICAN 9406's SDS lists "UV curing pipeline for CIPP (Cured In Place Pipe) applications" directly as a relevant identified use — the first primary-source confirmation in this workspace of a UV-curable polyester, as distinct from the vinyl-ester and epoxy UV products already catalogued in Lesson 44 (MERICAN 9515/9515N-01/9516N-01, MFE 775UV/752UV). Polyester is usually pitched as the economical default for standard sanitary/storm work (Lesson 6); this SDS shows the UV-cure option isn't confined to the vinyl ester/epoxy families this workspace had previously associated it with exclusively.
