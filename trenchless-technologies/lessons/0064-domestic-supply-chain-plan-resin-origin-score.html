Lesson 64: The Domestic Supply Chain Plan: How Resin Origin Enters the Score

For the first time in this workspace, a Toronto tender puts “CIPP Resin” on its own scored line — by where it was made.

Lesson 63 said the resin maker doesn’t have to be Canadian. This lesson shows the catch: the contractor must report where its resin came from, and that report earns or loses points.

What the form asks

Form F, the Domestic Supply Chain Plan (DSCP), is mandatory (Part 1 §1.9.6): leave it incomplete or unsigned and the bid is non-compliant. Its goods table lists four “Major Goods” lines — CIPP Resin, CIPP Felt, manhole rehabilitation materials (frames and covers, precast units, FRP ladders) and backfill materials. For each, the bidder splits the dollar value across three columns:

ColumnMeaning
AOntario-made goods
BCanadian-made goods (excluding anything already in A)
COther countries

The instructions also require services to be reported (Ontario services versus Canadian services). The Form F file I was given contains only the goods table; the instructions refer to Tables 1, 2 and 3, so the services and summary tables presumably sit in the City’s online system — I could not read them.

The definitions that decide the column

  • Ontario-made: wholly manufactured or originating in Ontario, or at least 51% of the total direct costs of producing the good were incurred in Ontario.
  • Canadian-made: wholly made in Canada, or at least 51% of direct costs incurred in Canada, or labelled “Made in Canada” / “Product of Canada”.
  • Services: counted only when provided by individuals located in Ontario (or elsewhere in Canada).

Each dollar goes in one column only; Ontario takes precedence. Double-reporting can cost the bidder its advantage (the bid itself stays compliant).

The formula

Domestic Content % = ( (A + A + B) ÷ Bid Price ) × 100, where A = Ontario-made goods plus Ontario services and B = Canadian-made goods plus Canadian services. Ontario value counts twice, Canadian value once, capped at 200%. The one bid with the highest percentage gets a notional 10% price reduction for ranking only — its submitted price and contract price do not change (Part 1 §2.1.1).

Ranking is otherwise by lowest price (Stage 2). So the DSCP is not a pass/fail gate; it only decides who gets the 10% advantage. Note also that the percentage divides by the whole bid price, so labour and site services — which a Toronto-area contractor delivers locally — carry most of the weight.

A hypothetical, to see the size of the effect

These dollar figures are invented for illustration; they are not from the tender. Take a $10.0M bid with $4.0M of Ontario services, $1.0M of Ontario manhole and backfill materials, $0.9M of Ontario-made felt, and $0.6M of resin. Only the resin’s origin changes:

Resin is…ABDomestic Content %
Imported (Column C)$5.9M$0(5.9 + 5.9 + 0) ÷ 10 = 118%
Canadian-made, outside Ontario$5.9M$0.6M(5.9 + 5.9 + 0.6) ÷ 10 = 124%
Ontario-made$6.5M$0(6.5 + 6.5 + 0) ÷ 10 = 130%

So resin origin moves the score by 6 to 12 points here — real, but small beside services. It matters only when two bids are close on domestic content and within about 10% on price, because only one bid gets the advantage.

Where the resin’s origin also shows up: the Pricing Form

Part 5, instruction 1.4, requires every supplier to state the country of origin of goods offered, to help the City “understand how Tariffs from the United States of America are being applied during the bidding process.” Part 1 defines country of origin as “the last country in which the goods were produced.” Pricing must be inclusive of all duties (except HST, itemized separately).

What this means for a Shanghai-made resin (my inference, not stated by the City). Resin shipped finished from China would land in Column C with no domestic credit, and its origin would be declared on the Pricing Form. That does not make a bid non-compliant. Whether any Canadian blending or packaging could reach the “51% of total direct costs” test is an open question — direct costs include raw materials, so it looks hard — and it is a good one to put to the City or to a prospective contractor before relying on it. The reference on setting up a Canadian company (see the company-setup cheat sheet) is relevant here, but nothing in the tender says a Canadian entity alone changes the resin’s classification.
