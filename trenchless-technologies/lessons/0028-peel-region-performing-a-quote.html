Lesson 28: Performing a Quote

Every clause in this package that actually moves a bid price — plus a real reconciliation calculation, worked step by step with Peel Region's own numbers.

A technical spec and a quote are different documents, but a good quote is built by reading the technical spec for exactly this: what's baked into the unit price by default, what gets billed separately, and what fixed rules govern anything that deviates from the base design.

Pay-item mechanics that shape how a bid should be structured

  1. Thickness increments are fixed, not exact. If a Field-Verified Design ends up needing a thicker liner than the Base-Bid Design assumed, extra thickness is only paid in three fixed increments: 1.5 mm, 3.0 mm, or 4.5 mm. A bid can't recover cost for "the exact extra 0.8mm actually needed" — it gets rounded into these bands. This means getting the Base-Bid Design thickness as close to right as possible up front matters more than in a pass-through-cost model.
  2. One day of cleaning is already "free." Where pre-tender CCTV wasn't available or complete, the Contractor is required to include the cost of one full 10-hour working day of reaming, cutting, and flushing in the base bid — only time beyond that day is separately billable. This is a real contingency cost every bid on a section without complete pre-tender CCTV needs to have already absorbed.
  3. Sampling and testing are their own line item. Not bundled into the lining unit price — priced and paid separately, per the Schedule of Quantities and Prices. Combined with the 100% sampling rate (below), this is a real, non-trivial cost line to price accurately, not an afterthought.
  4. Sampling rate is 100%, not 30%. Both the hot water/steam and UV specs require samples and testing for every liner run installation — stricter than OPSS.MUNI 443's generic 30% gravity-sewer rate (Lesson 23). The named testing agency is Paragon Systems, Concord, Ontario, "or equivalent" — a real, budgetable line item, not a rough allowance.
  5. CIPPSR has a hard thickness floor, independent of design. Every spot repair liner must reach at least 4.5 mm (felt) or 4.0 mm (UV-cured) — even if the structural design calculation would allow less. No design reconciliation can reduce this floor. This sets a real, non-negotiable minimum material cost per spot repair, regardless of how favorable the resin's properties are.

Time and equipment constraints that drive labor cost

  • CIPPSR's 4-hour rule: the sewer line and connection can be disrupted no more than 4 hours total across the entire process — inspection, cleaning, prep, and installation combined. This is a real crew-productivity constraint: a spot repair crew has to be fast, which affects crew size and equipment planning more than a mainline job with a longer disruption window.
  • Odour control equipment is specified, not optional: a minimum of two manhole exhaust fans at 2,100 cfm each, plus (for hot water/steam CIPPSR) a calibrated styrene monitor capable of reading parts-per-billion on site at all times. Both are real, priceable equipment line items — not a vague "provide adequate ventilation" allowance.
  • Public notification is scope-specific, not one blanket requirement. The supplemental spec's Table 9.1 sets separate notice protocols (14-day pre-construction, 24-hour service disruption, 24-hour odour notice) across four different scopes — mainline cleaning, mainline lining, open-cut replacement, and manhole work — each triggering its own notification cycle. A job touching multiple scope types has to plan for multiple, overlapping notification cycles, not one.

Risk-management clauses that affect bid pricing

  • Two-year warranty, with a formal exclusion path. If the Contractor believes existing pipe condition means a proper warranty can't be given, they must say so before installation, via a written Warranty Exclusion Letter the Contract Administrator has to approve — submitted after the liner is installed, it's automatically rejected. This is a real pre-bid risk decision: flag a questionable segment upfront, or accept full warranty exposure on it.
  • Excavated repairs are a quote-within-the-quote. When an excavated repair becomes necessary, the Contractor has to submit location, sewer size and depth, repair length, crew and equipment needs, subcontractors, time required, and an estimated cost — before getting approval to proceed. Worth having a template ready for this rather than building one from scratch mid-project.

Worked example: how design reconciliation actually changes a bid

These are Peel Region's own worked examples, reproduced in full because the mechanics genuinely matter for pricing risk.

Example 1 — restrained sample, no deficiency

ParameterAs designedReconciliation (using test results)
Flexural modulus, short-term2,413 MPa2,995 MPa (tested)
Flexural strength, short-term31 MPa38.9 MPa (tested)
Required liner thickness7.6 mm7.1 mm
Tested thickness7.2 mm
ConclusionThickness appears deficient (7.2mm < 7.6mm designed)Not deficient — the tested material properties were better than designed, lowering the actual required thickness to 7.1mm, which 7.2mm clears

The liner tested stronger than the design assumed, so the required thickness recalculates downward — and the as-built 7.2mm now clears the bar. Good news for the contractor, but only because the actual test came back favorable.

Example 2 — plate sample, deficient

ParameterAs designedReconciliation (using derated test results)
Flexural modulus, short-term2,758 MPa2,465 MPa tested → 2,095 MPa after 15% plate de-rating
Flexural strength, short-term31 MPa36.6 MPa tested → 29.2 MPa after 18% plate de-rating
Required liner thickness9.6 mm10.6 mm
Tested (field) thickness10 mm
ConclusionAppears deficientStill deficient — even after reconciliation, the required thickness (10.6mm) exceeds the actual installed thickness (10mm)
Two things worth noting for pricing risk. First, flat-plate samples get two different de-rating percentages here — 15% for modulus, 18% for strength — not one flat figure across both, a refinement worth knowing beyond the single "15%" figure from Lesson 23's OPSS.MUNI context. Second, this example shows reconciliation doesn't always save a borderline result — a plate sample that tests weaker than a restrained sample would (because it cures under different conditions) can turn an apparently-fine liner into a genuinely deficient one. Where possible, that's a real argument for pushing toward restrained cylindrical samples over plate samples when the choice is available.
