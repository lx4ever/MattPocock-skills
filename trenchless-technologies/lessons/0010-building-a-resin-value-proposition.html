Lesson 10: Building a Resin Value Proposition

A different buyer, a different pitch — grounded in what actually drives quality, performance, and cost.

Everything so far has been about explaining CIPP to a client buying a rehab job. This flips the audience: you're now the one selling a resin to contractors and municipal engineers who already know the category. A resin's value proposition has to connect to the real levers from Lesson 9 — not just claim to be "better."

Six value-proposition angles, each tied to a real lever

  1. Structural performance headroom. Not just "clears ASTM F1216" — by how much, and what does that mean for the 50-year creep-retention design assumption? A resin that holds flexural modulus better under long-term load supports a stronger design-life claim, which matters directly to the engineer signing off on the spec.
  2. Cure economics. Faster cure, or compatibility with more cure methods, means less downtime per job and more jobs per crew per week. This is the lever that turns into a contractor's bottom line fastest — and it's the easiest one to put a number on.
  3. Installation forgiveness. Viscosity, handling characteristics, and working-time window that reduce wet-out defects and give crews more margin for error in the field. Fewer callbacks and warranty claims is a real cost saving, even if it's harder to headline than "cures in 20 minutes."
  4. Environmental / regulatory profile. Low-styrene or styrene-free formulations reduce VOC exposure, odor complaints, and regulatory risk. This doesn't have to mean "fully styrene-free" — a real product in this space (EnviroCure) is positioned as safer and cheaper than full styrene-free alternatives while still improving on standard styrenated resin. There's room between the two extremes, not just at them.
  5. Broad compatibility. A resin that works across both felt and fiberglass tube, and multiple cure methods, lets a contractor stock one product instead of several — a real simplicity value, separate from any single performance number.
  6. Field-proven, third-party validated data. Buyers explicitly prioritize compliance testing and a track record across varied soil, climate, and flow conditions over a spec sheet claim. A new resin without case studies or independent test data is starting from a credibility deficit, no matter how good the chemistry is.
The reframe that matters most: buyers in this market think in total installed cost — material plus labor plus downtime plus warranty risk plus compliance exposure — not price per gallon. A resin priced above the category average can still win, if the pitch quantifies what it saves elsewhere. Leading with price-per-gallon plays a game you're likely to lose; leading with total installed cost changes the comparison entirely.

Match the buyer to the lever

Different buyers care about different angles first. Practice picking the lead.

Which value prop leads?

1. A design engineer is specifically asking about your resin's 50-year design-life performance, not its price.

2. A contractor wants to fit more jobs into each week without adding crews.

3. A city in a dense urban area has fielded resident odor complaints on past CIPP jobs and is now scrutinizing bids for environmental impact.

4. A contractor currently stocks two separate resin lines because their crews sometimes use felt tube with hot water cure, and sometimes fiberglass with UV cure.
