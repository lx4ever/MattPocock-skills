Lesson 59: Resin, Catalyst, and Hardener — Who Mixes What, and When

Lesson 15 named promoter, inhibitor, and initiator as the chemistry that drives cure speed. This lesson is the supply-chain and equipment question sitting right behind it: what actually ships from the manufacturer, what gets added on site, by whom, with what equipment — and why resin has a shelf life measured in months while a mixed batch has a pot life measured in minutes.

Three words that aren't interchangeable

Promoter (accelerator). A small dose — typically a cobalt salt such as cobalt naphthenate or cobalt octoate, usually supplied and referenced as a "6%" solution — added at roughly 0.1–1% of resin weight. Its job is to make the peroxide catalyst decompose fast enough, at ambient or moderate temperature, to actually trigger a cure. Promoter is pre-blended into the resin at the factory, alongside the inhibitor from Lesson 15 — it does not react with anything until catalyst is added.
Catalyst (initiator). A peroxide — MEKP (methyl ethyl ketone peroxide) is the industry-standard choice for polyester and vinyl ester — added at roughly 0.5–3% of resin weight, adjusted for the pot life and cure speed the job needs. This is the one component never added by the manufacturer. It's a small trigger dose, not a structural ingredient of the cured liner.
Hardener. A completely different role, specific to epoxy. MERICAN 3222 B (Lesson 58) — 100% tetrahydromethylphthalic anhydride — isn't a trace trigger dose at all; it's a full stoichiometric co-reactant that combines directly with the resin's epoxide groups, roughly molecule-for-molecule. That's why it ships as its own complete product (its own SDS, its own CAS number, its own hazard profile) rather than a small percentage additive folded into the resin drum the way catalyst is.
The one rule that explains why the resin drum arrives pre-promoted: promoter and catalyst must never be mixed directly with each other — contact between concentrated cobalt promoter and concentrated peroxide catalyst causes a violent, potentially explosive reaction. The only safe sequence is promoter blended into the resin first (at the factory, in bulk, under controlled conditions), with catalyst added last, directly into the already-promoted resin, in a small dose, immediately before use. That safety constraint is the actual reason the mixing step splits the way it does between manufacturer and contractor — it isn't an arbitrary supply-chain habit.

This directly explains Lesson 6's UV wet-out finding, from the other direction. Lesson 6 established that UV-cured tube gets wet out upstream at a factory and shipped shelf-stable for months, while hot-water/steam tube gets wet out by the installer immediately before insertion. This lesson is the mechanism behind that split: hot-water/steam resin only becomes perishable once catalyst is added, so catalyzation has to happen right before use. UV resin was never catalyzed with a heat- or chemical-triggered reaction to begin with — there's no trigger dose to add late, so wet-out can safely happen far upstream of the job site.
One inference worth flagging as an inference, not a confirmed fact: none of the five SDSs reviewed in Lesson 58, and nothing in Sino Polymer's own brochure, states where MERICAN 3222 A/B's two components actually get combined. The short-pot-life logic above (Lesson 47's "epoxy generally shorter pot life once mixed" point) suggests two-part epoxy likely follows the same on-site-mixing pattern as hot water/steam resin, for the same reason — but that's a reasonable inference from the chemistry, not something confirmed in a primary Sino Polymer document for this specific product.

Real dosing numbers, to make the ratios concrete

Sino Polymer's own SDSs and brochure don't disclose exact catalyst/promoter dosing rates or the 3222 A:B mix ratio — that level of detail lives in a TDS or an installer's mixing instructions, not a hazard-communication document. Published industry-standard figures for polyester/vinyl ester give a real sense of scale: MEKP catalyst at roughly 0.5–3% of resin weight, cobalt promoter at roughly 0.1–1% of resin weight, both adjustable for ambient temperature and the pot life a specific job needs. For two-component epoxy specifically, a published CIPP-application patent describes a 10:1 (weight/weight) resin-to-hardener ratio as one real, if illustrative, example of how differently the epoxy split can be proportioned compared to polyester/VE's small-percentage catalyst dose — underscoring that hardener is a bulk co-reactant, not a trace additive, in a way MEKP catalyst never is.

What actually determines shelf life — and why it's a different number from pot life

Shelf life is inhibitor concentration racing against storage temperature. Lesson 15 already established that inhibitors exist specifically to prevent premature radical formation. Heat accelerates exactly the same reaction the inhibitor is suppressing — which is why every one of the five real SDSs reviewed in Lesson 58 gives some version of the same storage instruction: keep the container tightly closed, in a dry, cool, well-ventilated place, away from heat, sparks, and open flame. Shelf life isn't a fixed clock ticking down — it's inhibitor capacity being consumed faster in a hot warehouse than a cool one.

MFE 7516's SDS lists its incompatible materials directly: peroxides, amines, sulfur compounds, heavy metal ions, alkalis, reducing agents, and oxidizing agents. That list isn't an arbitrary chemical-safety catalog — it's essentially the promoter/catalyst chemistry itself. Peroxide is the catalyst. Cobalt is a heavy metal ion. Amines are a common epoxy hardener class. Storing uncatalyzed resin anywhere near any of these isn't a generic hazard — it's the same premature-cure risk the inhibitor is fighting off, arriving from an uncontrolled outside source instead of a deliberate dose.

The practical version of this table: shelf life and pot life are not the same number, and confusing them is a real field risk. A drum of promoted, uncatalyzed resin can sit in a cool warehouse for months. The moment MEKP goes in, that same material's clock changes from months to minutes-or-hours, refrigeration or not. A wet-out crew treating "the resin is fine, it's only been a few days" as equally true before and after catalyzation would be reasoning from the wrong number.
