Lesson 69: Peel’s Buy Ontario Rule Is Pass/Fail — and Its Tariff Clauses

The same domestic-content idea is a pass/fail floor in Peel and a scored advantage in Toronto — and Peel adds a tariff-risk allocation.

Lesson 64 showed Toronto scoring where a resin was made. Peel’s tender uses the same definitions in a much gentler way, and says who pays if tariffs change after you bid.

Peel’s rule: a pass/fail floor

Instructions to Bidders, section 8. To meet the Municipal Buy Ontario Procurement Directive, a bid must commit that at least 51% of the Bid Price consists of Ontario-made goods and Ontario services and/or Canadian-made goods and Canadian services. It is evaluated pass/fail, and the lowest-priced compliant bid is recommended for award. Domestic Content % = DSCP Total Value ÷ Bid Price × 100.
  • The commitment table is in the online bidding system. The detailed Domestic Supply Chain Plan (DSCP, Appendix 5.31) is requested only from the lowest-priced compliant bidder, within 48 hours of the unofficial results, or from anyone the Region chooses. A late or misleading DSCP can make the bid non-compliant.
  • Definitions match Toronto’s (Lesson 64): Ontario-made or Canadian-made means wholly made there, or at least 51% of total direct production costs incurred there (Canadian-made also covers a “Made in Canada” label); services count only when provided by individuals located there.
  • The Region may rely on the DSCP without checking, but can ask for supporting documents, due in 48 hours.

A hypothetical to see what the floor means for resin

Invented numbers, not from the tender. A $10.0M bid with $4.0M of Ontario services, $1.0M of Ontario manhole and backfill materials and $0.9M of Ontario-made felt already reaches 59% domestic content. A $0.6M imported resin line changes nothing about passing. So under a flat 51% floor, resin origin matters only for a bid whose other domestic content is thin — a very different pressure from Toronto’s scored plan.

Tariff clauses (sections 7, 11 and 12)

  • Prioritizing Canadian goods: Peel Regional Council has endorsed prioritizing Canadian and other non-US goods and services over US ones while the 2025 US and retaliatory Canadian tariffs are in effect.
  • Price only the tariffs in force at closing (11.1). If new tariffs arrive later, the contractor must notify the Region within 15 calendar days with rates and affected products, and take reasonable steps to mitigate (other sources, earlier shipment).
  • Reimbursement (11.2): if goods can only come from a tariffed country and cannot ship in time, the Region reimburses reasonable direct costs, with documentary evidence; indirect costs and markups are not covered.
  • Tariff Impact table (12.2): bidders give a range — 0%, 0–10%, 10–20%, 20–30% or over 30% — for the share of price attributable to tariffs. It is confidential and not used in evaluation.
  • Surtax Remission Order: the document describes a US-surtax remission window of April 16 to October 16, 2025 and asks bidders to say whether goods are eligible. Applying for relief is the importer’s job.
What I could not determine. The tender does not say which imported goods are caught by any tariff, so I can’t say whether resin shipped from China attracts one. The clauses decide who bears new tariffs after closing and make the contractor price existing ones; for a resin importer, the question to ask a contractor is how resin duties and surtaxes are being priced into its tariff-impact answer.
