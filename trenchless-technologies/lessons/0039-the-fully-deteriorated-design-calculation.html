Lesson 39: The Fully-Deteriorated Design Calculation

Not the literal ASTM equation text — that needs a purchased copy of the standard to cite precisely. This is the actual sequence of inputs and decisions an engineering firm works through to get from "here's a pipe" to "here's a required thickness."

What this lesson is, and isn't. The inputs, their typical values, and the order-of-operations below are well-corroborated across multiple independent secondary sources. The literal buckling formula (exact exponents, exact algebraic form) is not reproduced here, because it wasn't independently verifiable against a primary source this session — WebFetch access to every candidate source was blocked. Where Lessons 26–28 already established a real number from a primary Peel Region document, that's flagged explicitly and should be trusted over anything in this lesson that isn't.

The inputs an engineer actually has to gather, before any calculation starts

  1. Host pipe geometry. Diameter, and depth to the pipe's crown (top) from ground surface — depth drives the groundwater and soil load terms directly.
  2. Groundwater height above the pipe. Not the same as burial depth — this is specifically how much standing groundwater the design has to assume sits above the pipe, since a fully deteriorated liner has to resist external hydrostatic pressure from that water column with zero help from the host pipe.
  3. Soil parameters. A typical soil density figure cited across sources is around 120 pounds per cubic foot, with a soil modulus (a measure of how much lateral support the surrounding soil provides) around 1,000 psi as a common design value — though real values should come from an actual geotechnical assessment of the specific site, not a generic assumption.
  4. Live load. Traffic loading over the pipe, typically expressed as HS-20 or H-20 highway loading — the standard design truck-load assumption used across North American pipe design generally, not something specific to CIPP.
  5. Ovality. The host pipe's actual measured out-of-roundness, expressed as a percentage — this comes from the CCTV/inspection data (Lesson 32), not an assumption. A commonly cited example design value is around 2%, but this should reflect what was actually measured on the specific segment.
  6. The liner's long-term material properties. Not the resin's initial (short-term) flexural modulus from a datasheet — the time-corrected value, found by applying a Creep Retention Factor to the initial modulus. A commonly cited example uses a 50% creep retention factor, meaning the design assumes the liner retains only half its initial stiffness over the design life. This is exactly the ASTM D2990 long-term creep testing flagged as an open item for Sino Polymer in the certification checklist (Lesson 22, reference doc) — without that test, there's no defensible number to put in this specific input. Update — Lesson 42: a real, third-party 50-year creep test now exists for MERICAN 9405T, at a measured retention factor of ~43% — close to, but not identical to, the illustrative 50% figure used here. Use the real number where it applies, and note it was measured via ISO 10468, not literally ASTM D2990.
  7. Poisson's ratio. A material property describing how a material deforms under load; commonly assumed at 0.3 for CIPP resin systems generically, absent product-specific test data.
  8. Enhancement factor K and safety factor N. Already covered in Lesson 19 and Lesson 38 — K in the empirical 3.6–21.4 range (7.0 as Peel's own conservative choice), N typically 2.0.

How these combine, conceptually

The governing external pressure the liner has to resist is built up from groundwater height, soil weight and lateral support, and live load — combined per the standard's own load-combination rules. That pressure gets compared against the liner's calculated buckling resistance, which is a function of its long-term modulus, its thickness, the enhancement factor, the ovality reduction factor, and the pipe's diameter. Because thickness appears inside that buckling-resistance calculation, the process is really: propose a thickness, calculate the resulting buckling resistance, check it against the required external pressure times the safety factor, and adjust the thickness until it clears with the desired margin — conceptually similar to how Lesson 28's worked reconciliation examples ran the same comparison in reverse, checking whether an as-tested thickness still satisfied the required design values after de-rating.

The direction every single input variable pushes in: deeper groundwater, denser soil, heavier live load, more ovality, a lower creep retention factor, or a lower K value — every one of these increases the required thickness. There's no input in this list where a worse real-world condition produces a thinner design requirement. That's worth having as an intuition check on a proposed design: if a segment has known bad conditions (high groundwater, poor soil, significant measured ovality) and the resulting design thickness looks thin, something in the inputs is worth double-checking before trusting it.

These two examples used different design flexural modulus inputs (2,413 vs. 2,758 MPa) and produced different required thicknesses (7.6mm vs. 9.6mm) for what the source material describes as comparable segments — a concrete, real illustration that the design flexural modulus assumption (itself downstream of the creep retention factor and the specific resin/liner system being designed around) is a real lever on the final thickness number, not just a paperwork input.

What actually gets ordered isn't the raw calculated number

Lesson 28 already established the practical endpoint: Peel bills extra thickness in fixed 1.5mm increments (Item D-7). A calculated minimum requirement of, say, 7.6mm doesn't get manufactured to exactly 7.6mm — the Base-Bid Design sets a baseline, and any Field-Verified Design increase gets rounded up to the next full 1.5mm unit. The structural calculation in this lesson produces the minimum defensible number; the actual liner thickness ordered and installed is that number rounded up to whatever increment the specific contract's pay-item structure uses.
