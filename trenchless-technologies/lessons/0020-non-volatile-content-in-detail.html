Lesson 20: Non-Volatile Content, In Detail

A quality-control number that turns out to be mechanically linked to shrinkage, styrene emissions, and what you're actually paying for.

Lesson 17 flagged non-volatile content as the property most pitches skip entirely. Here's why that's a mistake — it connects directly to three things that already showed up as major themes: cure defects (Lesson 9), styrene emissions (Lessons 9 and 12), and total installed cost (Lesson 10).

What it actually measures

The standard method is ASTM D2369 — heat a resin sample at 110±5°C for 60 minutes, and measure the mass lost. Whatever evaporates is the volatile fraction. Non-volatile content (percent solids) is what's left, calculated by difference. It's a direct, physical measurement of how much of what's in the can actually stays behind and becomes part of the cured liner.

The shrinkage connection

Volatile content that evaporates during cure doesn't just disappear cleanly — its departure is a major driver of cure shrinkage. This maps directly onto the resin-family comparison from Lesson 6:

  • Epoxy systems generally show the lowest shrinkage.
  • Vinyl ester sits in the middle.
  • Unsaturated polyester typically shows the highest polymerization shrinkage of the three.
Why this ranking exists: it tracks almost exactly with how much reactive volatile content (largely styrene monomer in conventional systems) each chemistry carries and loses during cure. Styrene-free or low-styrene systems are frequently described as "virtually shrink-free" compared to standard styrenated resin — non-volatile content and the styrene-content story from Lesson 17 aren't two separate facts. They're mechanically the same underlying behavior, described from two different angles.

Manufacturing and quality impact

  1. Batch-to-batch predictability. A certified, consistent non-volatile content means the resin behaves the same way at wet-out every time — predictable viscosity, predictable thickening behavior (Lesson 16), predictable cured wall thickness for a given applied volume.
  2. Shrinkage and void risk. More volatile content lost during cure means more shrinkage, which directly feeds the resin-void and delamination defect modes from Lesson 9. A resin with unpredictable non-volatile content is a resin with unpredictable defect risk, independent of how good its cured mechanical properties look on a datasheet.
  3. Dimensional accuracy. Shrinkage doesn't just create voids — it can pull the cured wall thickness below the design figure calculated in Lesson 19. A liner that shrinks more than expected may not actually deliver the wall thickness the buckling-resistance design assumed.

The cost angle: paying for what actually becomes product

This is the part a price-per-kilogram comparison misses entirely. If a resin's non-volatile content is lower, more of what's purchased and applied evaporates during cure and never becomes part of the finished liner. Two resins priced identically per kilogram are not the same value if one delivers meaningfully less solid content into the cured wall — the lower-non-volatile-content resin effectively costs more per unit of actual liner material delivered, even though the invoice looks the same.

Sales-relevant point: a certified non-volatile content figure is a real, checkable number — not a soft claim. It protects a buyer against exactly this kind of hidden cost, and it's a legitimate, specific thing to ask a competitor to produce if their resin is priced lower per kilogram. "Cheaper per kilogram" and "cheaper per liner" are different claims, and non-volatile content is the number that tells them apart.
