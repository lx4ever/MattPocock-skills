Lesson 4: How Fold-and-Form Reshapes a Pipe

No resin, no spray, no chemical cure — just a real pipe, folded small, then heated back round.

Recall fold-and-form's tell from Lesson 1: a solid thermoplastic pipe (PE or PVC), factory-folded into a compact H, U, or C shape, gets reshaped twice — once at the factory, once in the field. Neither CIPP's resin cure nor SIPP's adhesion applies here. The new pipe wall is mechanical: heat and pressure wedge a real pipe tight against the old one.

The six steps

  1. Inspect & clean. A CCTV run checks for obstructions and confirms the line is clear, then the pipe is cleaned.
  2. Soften & winch in. The folded liner, delivered on a coil, is conditioned in a steam cabinet until it's pliable, then winched through the host pipe by cable — pulled from one manhole to another, using two access points.
  3. Revert & form. Steam (commonly around 125°C) combined with compressed air reverts the folded liner back to round and expands it into close contact with the host pipe wall — a thermoforming process, not a chemical one.
  4. Cool & set. Compressed air or a water-air mix cools the liner until the exhaust temperature holds steady (commonly cited around 110°F) for roughly 20 minutes, locking the round shape in place.
  5. Trim the ends. Robotic cutters trim the liner at each access point, deliberately leaving a small overhang (roughly 75–150 mm) — that flare is what preserves the tight fit the reversion step created.
  6. Reinstate laterals & final inspection. Robotic cutters reopen every branch connection to a precise tolerance (roughly 90–105% of the original opening size, smooth and circular), then a final CCTV run confirms the whole line.
Sales-relevant point: fold-and-form's liner is manufactured 2–2.5% smaller in diameter than the host pipe, on purpose — the reversion step stretches it just enough to seat tightly without over-stressing the material or leaving inconsistent wall thickness. There's no adhesive and no cure chemistry involved at all. That's genuinely different from both CIPP and SIPP, and it's worth having ready: a client who's heard "cured-in-place" and "spray lining" pitched to them may assume every liner works the same way. Fold-and-form doesn't — it's closer to installing a second, slightly undersized pipe and heat-fitting it into place.

The trap: it looks like CIPP pull-in-place

Fold-and-form gets winched in from two access points, on a cable — exactly like CIPP's pull-in-place method from Lesson 2. If a client describes a job as "they winched a liner in from two manholes," that alone doesn't tell you which method it was. The real tell is what happens after insertion: CIPP's tube gets calibrated with pressure and chemically cured; fold-and-form's solid pipe gets thermoformed round with steam and air, then simply cooled. No resin appears anywhere in fold-and-form.

Quick check

Fold-and-form or CIPP pull-in-place?

1. A liner is winched in from two manholes, then air pressure holds it against the wall while it's chemically cured hard. Which method?

2. A liner is winched in from two manholes, then steam and compressed air revert it to round, and it's cooled until it holds shape. Which method?

3. Why is the fold-and-form liner manufactured 2–2.5% smaller than the host pipe diameter?
