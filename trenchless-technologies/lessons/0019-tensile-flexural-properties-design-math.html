Lesson 19: Tensile & Flexural Properties — The Real Design Math

Why flexural modulus (not tensile) actually sizes a liner's wall thickness — and a real finding that "stiffer is better" isn't always true.

Lesson 17 flagged that strength/modulus can trade off against wall thickness, and thickness against material cost. This lesson is the actual mechanism: the ASTM F1216 design equation these properties feed into, and a genuinely counterintuitive finding about how much stiffness is too much.

Tensile vs. flexural — why flexural is the one that matters here

Tensile testing (ASTM D638) pulls a sample along its axis. Flexural testing (ASTM D790) bends it — a three-point bend test. A buried pipe wall doesn't get pulled lengthwise; it gets bent, by soil pressure and groundwater pushing in from outside. Flexural properties model that loading mode directly, which is exactly why ASTM F1216's structural design equations are built on flexural modulus and flexural stress, tested per D790 — not the tensile figures that tend to get quoted more often.

The actual design equation, in plain terms

F1216 works from two design conditions:

  • Partially deteriorated: the liner and the old host pipe share the structural load together.
  • Fully deteriorated: the liner has to resist the full external buckling pressure — groundwater and soil load — entirely on its own, with zero assumed help from the host pipe.

Fully deteriorated is the more conservative case, and often the realistic one — the host pipe frequently needs CIPP because it can no longer be trusted to carry load. The design equation for this case is adapted from Timoshenko's classical buckling formula for a thin tube submerged in water, with an empirical correction factor, K, added to account for how much confinement the surrounding host pipe still provides even in a degraded state.

K isn't a clean constant. Research testing this on a 10-inch pipe found K ranging from 3.6 to 21.4 across 49 tests — a mean of 12.4, but real scatter. F1216 recommends a conservative minimum of K = 7 where full host-pipe support is confirmed. This is exactly why "the design meets F1216" is a real engineering judgment call involving field-verified assumptions, not a single number pulled off a datasheet.

Where modulus and thickness trade off — the cost lever

In this buckling formula, resistance to collapse scales with both flexural modulus (E) and wall thickness (t) together. A resin with a higher flexural modulus can hit the same buckling-resistance target with a thinner design wall. Less thickness means less total resin and tube consumed for the same job — a real material-cost saving that can offset a higher-modulus resin's higher price per kilogram. This is the mechanism behind the cost claim in Lesson 17 — not a vague gesture at "efficiency," but a specific term in a specific equation.

The counterintuitive finding: more stiffness isn't free

Documented research finding: excessively high elastic modulus (above roughly 60 GPa) combined with excessive thickness (above roughly 6.5 mm) can create severe stress concentration at the liner-host-pipe interface — and that can lead to liner cracking under cyclic loading over time. Stiffness and thickness aren't a "more is always better" pair. Past a point, pushing both up together creates a new failure mode at exactly the interface a design is trying to protect.

This is where elongation earns its place in the conversation. A liner needs enough flexibility to absorb cyclic stress — traffic loading, ground movement, thermal cycling — without brittle cracking at that interface. Elongation is what gives a stiff, high-modulus system the give it needs to survive real cyclic loading over decades, not just a single design-load calculation on day one.

Elongation's other job: the "locking effect"

Resin doesn't just coat the inside of the old pipe — it flows into existing cracks, gaps, and surface defects in the host pipe wall and cures there too, creating a mechanical "locking effect" that improves adhesion and overall system reliability. A resin with adequate elongation tolerates this interlocked, irregular geometry without cracking at those exact interface points during cure shrinkage or later service stress — tying directly back to the interface-stress finding above.
