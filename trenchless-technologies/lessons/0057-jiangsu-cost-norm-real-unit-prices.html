Lesson 57: The Jiangsu Cost Norm — Real Unit Prices, and What They Don't Tell You

A real, official Chinese provincial government cost-estimating standard for trenchless pipe rehab — a different kind of document from every Peel or Toronto source reviewed so far, with a genuine price comparison and an equally genuine honesty trap built into comparing it naively.

Two documents, both issued by 江苏省住房和城乡建设厅 (the Jiangsu Provincial Department of Housing and Urban-Rural Development): "附录N 江苏省城镇排水管道非开挖修复工程量清单" (Appendix N: Jiangsu Urban Drainage Pipeline Trenchless Rehabilitation Bill-of-Quantities), which defines the item classification codes, measurement units, and scope of work for every trenchless method; and "江苏省城镇排水管道非开挖修复工程估价表" (Jiangsu Urban Drainage Pipeline Trenchless Rehabilitation Engineering Pricing Table), an 83-page document giving the actual itemized unit price — labour, material, and machine cost broken down line by line — for each method at each diameter. Together they're a real cost norm, not a bid or a design spec.

First, the category distinction that matters most

Every primary source in this workspace up to now has been either a design/material spec (OPSS.MUNI 443, Peel's Appendix 5.9/5.10, Toronto's TS 4.10/D5813) or a live competitive procurement package (Peel's and Toronto's actual RFTs). This is neither. It's a government-published cost norm — a standardized reference for how much labour, material, and machine time a given unit of work is assumed to consume, priced at a stated regional price level (explicitly "2024年下半年南京市价格口径," H2 2024 Nanjing pricing). It's the kind of document used to build a tender ceiling price (招标控制价) or an internal cost estimate, not a document a bidder submits or a City enforces materials minimums through. There's no flexural modulus, no ASTM/ISO citation, no creep retention factor anywhere in it — because it isn't answering "is this liner strong enough," it's answering "how much does this cost to build."

The real finding worth having as a talking point: UV cure costs more than hot water cure at every matching diameter in this real government cost norm, not less. At DN300, UV runs about 15% above hot water; the gap narrows at larger diameters but UV never comes in cheaper. This is a genuinely useful, primary-sourced data point against the common assumption that UV's speed advantage automatically nets out to a lower total price — in Jiangsu's own official cost structure, UV's speed and quality advantages come at a real, quantified material/equipment cost premium, not a savings.
Spiral-wound lining is the standout expensive outlier — roughly 5–7x the per-metre cost of hot water CIPP at a comparable diameter. The reason is visible directly in the material line items: a spiral-wound job consumes real quantities of PVC-U profile strip (¥163/m unit price alone) and stainless-steel banding, a fundamentally different material-cost structure from a resin-saturated felt tube. Worth knowing if spiral-wound (a real method in Toronto's own OPSS.PROV 466/467 sliplining family, Lesson 18) ever comes up as a comparison point against CIPP specifically on cost grounds.

The clearest new finding: pipe bursting is expensive relative to CIPP in this cost norm, not cheap. A method often framed as a lower-cost alternative to lining — because it replaces the pipe outright rather than building a composite liner — actually costs 2–3x hot water CIPP's per-metre price at DN300 in Jiangsu's own official numbers, driven entirely by specialized pulling/expansion equipment costs rather than material. Short-pipe (HDPE) lining, by contrast, genuinely is the cheapest full-rehabilitation option at small diameters — a real, useful distinction between two methods that both fall under "sliplining" in this workspace's broader taxonomy (Lesson 18) but land in very different cost brackets.
A repeated material pattern worth flagging: polyurethane, not epoxy or acrylic, is this cost norm's default chemistry for grouting and leak sealing. Both the structural chemical-grouting item (2.1.2) and the joint leak-sealing item (2.2) use "高效聚氨酯" (high-efficiency polyurethane) materials specifically. That's a real, consistent chemistry choice across an entire chapter of this document — worth knowing if a resin conversation ever needs to compare Chinese grouting-material practice against the North American chemical-grouting chemistry already covered in Lesson 30.

A real, structural difference from both Peel and Toronto: no styrene-free pricing tier at all

Toronto's real 2025 pricing structure (Lesson 52) separately prices styrene-free resin as its own pay item, cutting across every diameter and road class. Peel's own specs (Lesson 27) mandate non-styrene resin outright for storm sewers under specific conditions. This Jiangsu cost norm has no resin-chemistry variable anywhere in its pricing structure — hot water CIPP and UV CIPP are priced purely by diameter, with no styrene-free/standard split at all. That's a genuine, structural difference worth being precise about: it doesn't necessarily mean styrene management isn't a real concern in Chinese practice — the item classification document (Appendix N) doesn't capture material-chemistry variables the way it captures method and diameter — but it does mean this specific cost norm can't be used to benchmark a styrene-free cost premium the way Toronto's real tender data can.

Now there are three genuinely different kinds of "CIPP cost" number in this workspace, and none of them convert cleanly into each other. The Jiangsu figures are a government labour/material/machine cost norm, explicitly excluding the province's own stated 19–20% management fee and 10% profit markup — a bare cost floor, not a price a customer pays. The Toronto/GTA retail figures are the opposite: a homeowner-facing all-in price already including contractor markup, mobilization, camera inspection, and permits, for small-diameter residential laterals (typically 100–150mm), not large-diameter municipal mainline. And Peel's/Toronto's real municipal tender data (Lessons 26–28, 51–52) never disclosed actual submitted bid dollar figures at all — those documents show the pricing structure a bidder fills in, not the competitive numbers themselves, which aren't public. Lining up the Jiangsu DN300 hot-water figure (≈CAD $124/m) against the Toronto retail figure (≈CAD $262–820/m) might suggest North American CIPP costs 2–6x the Chinese cost-norm floor — but that gap is diameter, currency, markup-inclusion, and market-type all bundled together, not a clean read on any single one of them.

What this document is genuinely useful for — and what it isn't

  • Useful for: understanding the real labour/material/machine cost structure behind a Chinese trenchless job, at a granular, itemized level (down to individual bladder/plug SKUs, resin-tube-per-metre material costs, and machine-hour rates) — genuinely rare visibility into cost composition that neither Peel's nor Toronto's procurement documents disclose (those show what a bidder is asked to price, not the cost buildup behind a competitor's number).
  • Not useful for: a direct "is China cheaper than Ontario" conclusion. This is a government-published cost norm reflecting Jiangsu regional labour rates (¥130–330/day, roughly CAD $25–62/day — a small fraction of Ontario construction wages) and material costs at a single regional price point, not a competitive bid price the way Peel's or Toronto's actual submitted tenders are. The gap between this document's numbers and a real Ontario bid price reflects currency, regional labour-cost differences, and the norm-vs-competitive-bid distinction all at once — not a clean read on relative efficiency or material cost alone.
