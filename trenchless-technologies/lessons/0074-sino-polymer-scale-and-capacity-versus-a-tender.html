Lesson 74: Sino Polymer’s Scale and Capacity, Set Against a Real Tender

The profile and catalogue describe a large, ISO-certified manufacturer — and the arithmetic shows scale is not what a tender turns on.

Two Sino Polymer marketing documents are new to this workspace. Before reading the CIPP products in them, take the company-level claims and test them against a real job’s size.

The two documents

The 2025 Company Profile is a 49-page slide deck with extractable text. The 2024 Catalogue is two image-only pages (four panels), so I read it by looking at rendered pages rather than copying text; table cells below were read by eye. Neither is independent evidence — both are Sino Polymer’s own marketing — and neither contains a CIPP-specific test result.

A check on the headline number. The nine lines of the Shanghai capacity table sum to about 74,500 t. The profile’s four named partner plants (Italy 30,000, Saudi/North Africa 40,000, India region 10,000, Southeast Asia 30,000) add 110,000 t. 74,500 + 110,000 = 184,500, close to the 180,000 headline — so the headline appears to include partner plants, not only Shanghai. That is my arithmetic, not a statement the documents make, but it matters for Lesson 75: “180,000 t” is not all made in Shanghai.

Is capacity ever the constraint? An order-of-magnitude check

Assumptions invented for illustration: take Peel’s 32 lined segments (Lesson 68: 2,046.8 m, 750–1200 mm) and give every liner the same round-number 12 mm wall, which is not the real design thickness (Lessons 39–40 show how that is found). Wall area is the circumference times length: about 6,200 m² for the whole job. At 12 mm that is about 75 m³ of cured liner. At roughly 1.1 t per m³, the finished liner weighs about 80 t, and resin is only part of that, since the felt is part of the weight. So resin for the entire job is at most about 80 t — four 20-foot containers of IBCs at 20 t each (Lesson 60), and well under 1% of the 20,000 t annual vinyl-ester capacity stated for Shanghai.

The conclusion holds even if the assumed thickness is off by a factor of two: capacity is not what decides a municipal CIPP tender. What decides it is the paperwork in Lesson 76 and the delivery questions in Lesson 75.

What the scale claims do and don’t prove. They support “a large, established, ISO-certified manufacturer that can supply a job like this without strain”. They do not say anything about Ontario installations, long-term creep data, or CIPP samples tested by an accredited lab — the things Form E and Peel’s Appendices 5.14/5.15 actually ask for.
