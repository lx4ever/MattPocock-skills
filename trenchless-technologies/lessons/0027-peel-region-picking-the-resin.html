Lesson 27: Picking the Resin

The generic Ontario floor from Lesson 22 is a floor. Peel Region's actual bar is meaningfully higher — and it's different for reinforced versus non-reinforced liners.

This is the single most important correction this workspace has made to date. The Sino Polymer certification checklist (an earlier reference) compared their resin data against OPSS.MUNI 443's generic 1,724 MPa / 31 MPa floor. Peel Region's actual bid specification sets its own, higher numbers — and explicitly says the generic figure doesn't apply here.

The document says this explicitly: "ASTM F1216 minimum of 1724 MPa is not applicable." Peel Region isn't citing the generic ASTM/OPSS floor and adding to it — they're replacing it outright with their own, higher non-reinforced minimum (2,400 MPa, about 39% above the generic figure), and a reinforced minimum (8,000 MPa) more than 4.6 times the generic floor.

What this actually means for a real resin

Recall from Lesson 19: flexural modulus is a composite liner property, not purely a resin property — fiberglass reinforcement can carry a composite's stiffness well beyond what the neat resin alone would suggest. Sino Polymer's VE resins, on their own datasheets, run roughly 2,900–4,100 MPa (Lesson 11). Checked against Peel Region's actual numbers:

  • Against the 2,400 MPa non-reinforced bar: comfortably clears, with real margin.
  • Against the 8,000 MPa reinforced bar: the neat resin numbers alone don't get close — and that's expected, not a problem. An 8,000 MPa composite is a fiberglass-tube-plus-resin system result, not a neat-resin number. This is exactly the "neat resin vs. composite liner" gap flagged in Lessons 17 and 22–23, now with concrete figures showing how large that gap actually is: a ~3,000–4,000 MPa resin and an 8,000 MPa required composite aren't in conflict — they're different measurements of different things.

The real, actionable consequence: composite-liner test data (the actual cured fiberglass-tube-plus-Sino-Polymer-resin result) is what a Peel Region bid needs — not the resin datasheet, and not even the generic OPSS.MUNI comparison from the earlier checklist.

The enhancement factor: a direct payoff of Lesson 19

Peel Region's design tables specify an Enhancement Factor of 7.0. This is the same concept as the empirical K-factor from Lesson 19 — the correction term in the buckling design equation accounting for how much confinement the host pipe still provides. Lesson 19 cited a real research range of 3.6 to 21.4 (mean 12.4), with ASTM F1216 itself recommending a conservative minimum of 7 where full host-pipe support is confirmed. Peel Region's actual bid specification uses exactly that conservative floor. This isn't a coincidence — it's what "conservative, defensible engineering" looks like in a real procurement document.

A resistance-factor finding that favors fold-and-form

The non-circular design method (ASCE MOP 145, from Lesson 26) assigns different flexural strength resistance factors depending on how the liner is manufactured and cured:

Liner typeResistance factor (Φ)
CIPP resin impregnated and cured on-site0.8
CIPP resin impregnated off-site, cured on-site0.85
Factory-manufactured thermoplastic (PVC/PE), installed fold-and-form1.0 — no reduction
Why this matters: a resistance factor below 1.0 is a built-in design penalty — the design has to assume the material won't fully deliver its rated strength, to account for field-condition variability. Fold-and-form (Lesson 4) gets no such penalty here, specifically because it's factory-manufactured with tighter process control than field-cured CIPP. This is a genuine, quantified engineering reason a design might favor fold-and-form over CIPP for a non-circular pipe — not a marketing claim, a resistance factor written into the actual design methodology.

A resin choice the spec makes for you: storm sewers

Appendix 5.16: "Under no circumstances shall any liner installation process water... be discharged into a storm sewer or any other direct connection to surficial drainage courses or facilities. Where this cannot be feasibly achieved, the Contractor shall include the cost of non-styrene, VOC-free resin for lining of storm sewers." If discharge control isn't achievable on a given storm sewer job, styrene-free resin isn't a value-add pitch (Lesson 10's environmental lever) — it's the only compliant choice. This is precisely the scenario MFE 701-01T or MERICAN 9515N-01 (Lesson 11) were built for.
Refinement, from the actual live bid form (Lesson 29): this doesn't apply to every storm sewer segment in the contract. It's carved out as its own specific pay item — "Item C-4: Structural Rehabilitation of Existing Storm Sewer... Using UV or Styrene Free" — covering exactly two identified segments (23m on Dixie Rd, 15.1m on The Gore Rd), separate from the general "Item C-3" CIPP work covering the rest of the storm sewer scope. The clause is real and binding, but read the bid item list before assuming it's a blanket rule for an entire storm sewer contract — it's tied to specific segments where discharge control is known to be a problem.

A test-method nuance worth knowing

For reinforced/UV-cured liners specifically, the spec states: "ISO 11296 is the preferred test method for flexural properties of reinforced UV-CIPP as it better captures the effect of the reinforcing than the ASTM D790 method," and explicitly notes ASTM D790 tends to produce lower results than ISO 11296 for the same reinforced material — because ISO 11296 tests a curved coupon that reflects the liner's actual geometry, while D790 tests a flat coupon that doesn't capture the reinforcement's real contribution as well. For a reinforced liner near the passing threshold, which test method gets used can be the difference between passing and failing.
