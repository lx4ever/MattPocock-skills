Lesson 9: CIPP — What Actually Drives Quality, Performance, and Cost

Six process factors, and how resin and tube choice each pull on quality, performance, and cost differently.

Lesson 6 covered what materials exist. This lesson is about what makes a CIPP job actually turn out well — or fail — and where resin and tubing decisions specifically move the needle versus where execution does.

Six process factors that make or break a job

  1. Host pipe prep. Skimping on cleaning or drying is the leading cause of delamination — the liner separating from the host pipe wall — because the resin never got a clean, dry surface to key into.
  2. Wet-out completeness. Any dry spot in the tube becomes a resin void: a weak point with less structural capacity than the design assumed. This is a quality-control step, not a material property — it's about execution, whatever resin is used.
  3. Calibration & insertion technique. Poor calibration causes wrinkles, folds, and trapped air. It also affects ovality — how well the cured liner actually conforms to the host pipe's true shape. Field studies removing installed CIPP samples have measured deformation up to 23% of nominal diameter, against a 3% figure commonly used in the structural design calculation. That's not a small rounding error — it's a real gap between design assumption and field reality, driven by execution quality.
  4. Cure temperature uniformity. If hot water or steam temperature isn't held steady end-to-end, the resin can cure unevenly — leaving blisters, voids, or under-cured sections with less strength than spec.
  5. Post-cure testing. ASTM F1216 sets minimum structural targets — commonly cited figures are a flexural strength of at least 4,500 psi and a flexural modulus of at least 250,000 psi, tested on flat plate samples cured alongside the actual liner. Long-term performance is even more conservative: engineers typically design around a "creep retention factor" of about 50% of the initial flexural modulus, extrapolated to a 50-year service life — the material is assumed to weaken under sustained load over decades, and the design has to hold up anyway.
  6. Emissions during cure. Styrene-based resins release VOCs during curing — peak concentrations inside an active work zone have been measured as high as 25.5 ppm, and styrene is detectable by smell at just 0.16 ppm. This isn't federally regulated specifically for CIPP, but it falls under Clean Air Act VOC/hazardous-air-pollutant rules, and some state and local rules go further. This is a real cost driver: community complaints, regulatory exposure, and worker safety — not just a chemistry footnote.

Where resin choice moves the needle

DimensionResin's effect
Structural performanceChemistry sets the baseline flexural strength/modulus — different resin systems clear ASTM F1216's minimums by very different margins.
Chemical/corrosion resistanceEpoxy and vinyl ester generally hold up better than polyester in aggressive chemical environments — directly affects service life in those pipes specifically.
Cure speedResin chemistry has to be compatible with the intended cure method (ambient, heat, or UV/photoinitiated) — this is a major labor-cost and downtime lever, not just a technical detail.
Emissions & compliance costStyrene-based systems are cheaper up front but carry VOC/odor/regulatory exposure; styrene-free or low-styrene systems cost more per gallon but reduce that exposure.

Where tubing choice moves the needle

DimensionFeltFiberglass
FlexibilityBetter at tight bends and irregular host pipesStiffer, less forgiving of sharp geometry
Resin consumptionAbsorbs more resin per job (more material cost, more room for wet-out variability)Adds structural strength independent of the resin, which can allow a thinner overall design
Cure compatibilityWorks with ambient, hot water, or steam — not UV (opaque)Required for UV cure (light needs to pass through)
Net cost effectLower material cost, more field-execution riskHigher material cost, often lower labor/downtime cost via UV cure
The real point: resin and tube aren't separate cost levers — they're one system, evaluated together with the cure method. A cheaper resin with a felt tube might lose to a pricier UV-compatible fiberglass system once you account for labor hours and downtime saved. "Cheapest material" and "cheapest installed job" are frequently different answers.

Quick check

Find the root cause

1. Post-install inspection finds the liner has delaminated from the host pipe in several spots. Most likely root cause?

2. A removed liner sample shows ovality deformation well beyond the 3% figure used in the original design calc. What does that point to?

3. Residents near a job site file odor complaints during a hot-water cure. What's the most direct lever to reduce this on the next job?
