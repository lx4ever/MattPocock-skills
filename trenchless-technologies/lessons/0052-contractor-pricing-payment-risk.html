Lesson 52: Building a Contractor's Bid — Pricing, Payment, and Real Risk

The actual pay-item structure, how and when a contractor gets paid, and three real financial risks baked into this specific contract that a generic CIPP price-per-metre estimate would miss.

Lesson 51 covered how the bid package is structured and evaluated. This is what a contractor actually builds a number around — the pay items, the cash-flow timing, and the risk allocation the City has written into this specific contract, not a generic industry assumption.

The pay-item structure — four parts, and a real 2x2 pricing matrix

Part 3's Section 4 (Special Provisions) lays out the contract items in four groups:

  • Part A — Mobilization and Special Site-Specific Liner Design (SSLD). Mobilization is capped at 2% of total tender value, paid lump sum pro-rated across the contract duration. SSLD is a separate paid item for engineered, site-specific designs required when actual field conditions (invert depth, ovality, live load) exceed the parameters the standard design already accounts for — a real, billable escalation path when a segment turns out to need more than the default design.
  • Part B — Mainline CIPP lining, in a genuine 2x2 matrix. Items are split by diameter, then cross-cut by standard vs. styrene-free resin and by local vs. major arterial road. A given diameter can appear in up to four separate priced line items depending on those two variables — arterial-road work and styrene-free resin each carry their own distinct price, not a blanket surcharge.
  • Part C — Standalone repairs not tied to a full lining run: TPR/CIPPSR spot repairs, chemical grouting, flushing/debris removal, manhole rehabilitation, and one standalone full-length open-cut replacement.
  • Part D — Provisional items: increased liner thickness (in defined increments), paid-duty police, traffic control personnel, after-hours/weekend crew premiums, temporary bypass supply and operation, building service bypass, flow monitoring, and manhole frame/cover work.
Why the 2x2 matrix matters for estimating, not just billing. Because styrene-free resin and arterial-road work are each separately priced, a contractor's estimate has to track which specific combination applies to each of the 305 segments in the repair list — not apply one blended unit price across the whole job. Lesson 54 covers exactly how a segment ends up needing styrene-free resin, including segments that aren't flagged for it up front.

Getting paid — a real, four-stage milestone schedule

Mainline CIPP lining items don't pay out as a single lump sum on completion. Part 3 sets a specific milestone split:

Milestone% of item value paid
V1 CCTV acceptance (pre-lining inspection)5%
Pipe preparation complete + V2 CCTV acceptance25%
Liner installed + V3 CCTV acceptance50%
Full QA/QC acceptance (test report, wet-out sheet, curing log)20%

Standalone lump-sum repair items (Part C) use a simpler three-stage split instead: 10% on submittal, 70% on installation, 20% on restoration. Either way, the last chunk of payment on any lining item is explicitly gated on the QA/QC paperwork — the liner test report, the wet-out sheet, the curing log — being submitted and accepted, not just on the physical work being done.

Three real financial risks this contract writes in, by name

1. Liquidated damages — two separate clauses, easy to conflate. A $3,500 per calendar day Delay Liquidated Damages clause (GC 7.6.1, in the general Construction Agreement) applies to general schedule slippage past the Scheduled Date for Substantial/Total Performance. A separate $3,500 per working day clause (Section 4.2, in the special provisions) applies specifically to missing a named Priority Repair's completion date or the 100-working-day Interim Completion Date. These are not the same clause with two names — a contractor needs to know which schedule failure triggers which one, since "calendar day" and "working day" produce genuinely different dollar exposure over the same delay.
2. A real quantity discrepancy the City has already declined to fix. Addendum 4 records a bidder directly flagging that two Pricing Form line items (2,700m and 6,170m) don't match the sum of the corresponding segments in the repair list (2,669.5m and 6,151.2m — 31m and 19m short). The City's answer: the Pricing Form quantities stay as published, because of "potential variance in the pipe's GIS length compared to the actual installation length." That's a real, acknowledged measurement-basis risk the City is deliberately leaving with the bidder, not correcting — worth pricing a contingency for, not assuming away.
3. A layered holdback and bond stack that affects cash flow, not just final payout. On top of the 10% statutory Lien Holdback (Construction Act, released after Substantial Performance), the Construction Agreement adds a 2% Contractual Holdback retained from every proper invoice, plus a separate 2% Warranty Holdback held until the 2-year Warranty Period expires. A performance bond and a labour-and-material payment bond are each required at 50% of the total contract value. None of these percentages are unusual individually for a municipal contract, but stacked together they represent real capital tied up for the duration of a multi-year relationship with this one client — a genuine cash-flow planning input, not just a compliance checkbox.

Insurance — the floor, not a negotiable extra

The Construction Agreement requires coverage per GC 8.1 (excluding only Professional, Aviation, Watercraft, and Marine — not applicable to this scope), with specific named minimums where they apply elsewhere in the GC suite: $5M automobile liability, $2M professional liability where applicable, $5M aviation and watercraft liability where used. These aren't negotiable line items in a bid — Schedule A's Information Sheet locks the required coverage in before a bid is even submitted.
