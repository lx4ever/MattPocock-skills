Lesson 31: How Peel Actually Pays a Contractor

Two documents underneath everything in Lessons 26–30: the province-wide base contract terms, and Peel's own amendments on top of them. This is the layer that decides when money actually moves — and it sharpens the subcontractor/supplier finding from Lesson 29 in a way worth knowing.

Every technical spec read so far (Appendices 5.9–5.18) sits inside a legal skeleton: OPSS.MUNI 100, the standard "OPS General Conditions of Contract" used province-wide, amended by Peel's own Supplemental General Conditions. Neither document mentions CIPP once — but both control how a contractor (or a resin supplier one step removed) actually gets paid, and what a warranty commitment costs in real cash flow.

A two-layer structure, same pattern as before

This is the same layering trick as the OPSS.PROV/OPSS.MUNI split from Lesson 18, but for contract law instead of technical specs: OPSS.MUNI 100 (November 2006) is the generic base — used, unmodified, across Ontario municipal public works of every kind. Peel's Supplemental General Conditions Vol. 2.1 (revised January 2022) then amends specific clauses of that base for Peel's own contracts specifically. Reading either document alone gives an incomplete — sometimes wrong — picture of the actual terms.

The document precedence order, in full (Peel's revised version, SG.6): Agreement → addenda → Special Provisions → Contract Drawings → Ontario Provincial Standard Drawings → Ontario Provincial Standard Specifications → Ontario Provincial Supplemental Specifications → these Supplemental General Conditions → OPSS.MUNI 100 (2006) → Region of Peel Electrical Standards → Region of Peel Materials Specifications and Standard Drawings → Instructions to Bidders → appendices → Tender → Region of Peel Standard Contract Documents (Volume 2) → Hydro Electrical Standards → M.T.O. Traffic Control Manuals → M.T.O. Roadside Safety Manual.

Notice where Peel's Supplemental General Conditions sit: above OPSS.MUNI 100. Whenever the two disagree, Peel's version wins — which is exactly why the numbers below (not the 2006 base document's numbers) are the ones that actually apply on a Peel job.

The payment cycle

Peel pays monthly, tied to a "Proper Invoice" under Ontario's Construction Act — a defined legal term, not just an invoice that looks complete. A Proper Invoice must include the contractor's name/address, the billing period, the work description and quantities, the amount payable, a statutory declaration that subcontractors have been paid, WSIB clearance, and (a Peel-specific addition) documentation proving compliance with Ontario's Excess Soil Regulation, if applicable. Payment is due within 28 days of a compliant Proper Invoice — and Peel added a full Construction Act Adjudication clause (a 2019 Ontario reform) letting either party trigger binding interim adjudication over a payment dispute during the project, separate from and faster than the older mediation/arbitration process for other disputes.

Two-stage statutory holdback release

Ontario's Construction Act requires a statutory holdback (typically 10% of contract value) to be retained and released in two stages: once at Substantial Performance, once at final Completion. OPSS.MUNI 100's base default is a 46-day release window after each milestone is published; Peel's Supplemental Conditions extend both windows to 61 days. That's a real, quantifiable cash-flow fact for anyone financing work (or extending credit) against a Peel contract — the money sits an extra two weeks past the provincial default before it's releasable.

Peel also raised the dollar thresholds that define "Substantial Performance" itself: the base OPSS.MUNI 100 test uses a sliding scale starting at $500,000; Peel's version doubles that starting tier to $1,000,000, meaning a larger punch-list value is tolerated before a project counts as substantially complete on a Peel job than the province-wide default would allow.

For anyone estimating a real job's cash-flow timeline — including a resin supplier extending payment terms to a contractor — this is the actual shape of what stays locked up, for how long, and under what conditions it comes back.

The markup schedule for anything billed as a change order

Extra Work, Additional Work, and quantity changes beyond the negotiated tender price get billed on a Time and Material (Force Account) basis, under OPSS.MUNI 100's clause GC 8.02.04 — with fixed markup percentages set out clause by clause, not negotiated case by case:

The one Peel actually changed — and exactly where it changed it. OPSS.MUNI 100's base subcontractor markup, at GC 8.02.04.08.01, is tiered: "a) 20% of the first $3,000; plus b) 15% of the amount from $3,000 to $10,000; plus c) 5% of the amount in excess of $10,000." Peel's Supplemental General Conditions, at SG.40 ("Payment for Work by Subcontractors – GC 8.02.04.08"), amend this surgically: "In Clause GC 8.02.04.08.01, replace everything following 'calculated on the following basis:' with 'of 5% of the Cost of the Work on a Time and Material Basis.'" That's the entire amendment, verbatim — a one-clause edit that collapses a three-tier schedule into a single flat rate. Worth noting what SG.40 doesn't touch: the Labour, Material, and Equipment markups above (GC 8.02.04.04–.06) are untouched base OPSS.MUNI 100 figures — Peel only amended the subcontractor markup, not the direct-cost markups. A neighbouring clause, SG.39, only redefines "Working Time" for clarity and doesn't change any percentage.

Sharpening Lesson 29's flow-down finding: why "supplier" was named separately from "subcontractor"

OPSS.MUNI 100 defines Subcontractor narrowly: "a person, partnership or corporation undertaking the execution of a part of the Work by virtue of an agreement with the Contractor." A material or resin supplier who ships product but doesn't perform installation work doesn't naturally fit that definition — under ordinary contract-law reading, a supplier is not a subcontractor.

This makes the Lesson 29 finding sharper, not weaker. The bid form's origin-preference clause explicitly bound the winning bidder to "subcontractors and suppliers" — two separate, deliberately named categories. If "Subcontractor" alone would have been enough to sweep in a resin supplier, there'd have been no need to add "suppliers" as its own word. The drafting itself is evidence that Peel anticipated material suppliers might otherwise fall outside a pure "Subcontractor" reading, and closed that gap on purpose. A resin supplier one step removed from the general contractor isn't exempt through a legal technicality — the clause was written specifically to prevent that reading.

Separately, the base document requires the Contractor to notify the Contract Administrator 10 days before starting any subcontracted work, naming the intended subcontractor; the Administrator then has 5 days to accept or reject that subcontractor in writing. A resin supplier feeding into a contractor's liner system wouldn't necessarily be captured by this specific notice mechanic (since they're a supplier, not a Subcontractor, under the strict definition) — but the origin-preference obligation applies regardless, per the clause above.

The legal mechanism behind Lesson 29's OHSMS finding

Peel's Supplemental Conditions designate the winning Contractor as the "Constructor" under Ontario's Occupational Health and Safety Act — not just informally responsible for safety, but legally holding the OHSA's full constructor obligations, including the authority to remove any other contractor from the site for non-compliance with its safety program, even ones the Contractor didn't directly hire. This is the actual legal hook behind the mandatory OHSMS certification (ISO 45001, CSA Z45001, or IHSA COR) flagged in Lesson 29 — it isn't a soft preference, it's what lets Peel hold one legally accountable party responsible for every trade working the site.

One more modern compliance layer, briefly

Peel's Supplemental Conditions weave in Ontario's Excess Soil Regulation (O. Reg. 406/19) throughout — definitions, indemnification, disposal, and Proper Invoice documentation requirements all reference it. It governs how excavated soil must be tracked, managed, and disposed of, and it applies to any excavation-based work on this contract (equipment retrieval digs, maintenance hole work, open-cut segments) — worth knowing it exists as a real, separate 2019 Ontario compliance obligation layered on top of the technical liner specs, even though it isn't CIPP-specific.
