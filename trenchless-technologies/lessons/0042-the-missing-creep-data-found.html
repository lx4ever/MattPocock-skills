Lesson 42: The Missing Creep Data, Found

Since Lesson 22, the certification checklist has flagged one item as "open — not present in the brochure": long-term creep testing. A newly reviewed Sino Polymer technical deck answers it directly, with real third-party numbers.

The headline finding: in May 2013, a Sino Polymer customer sent a MERICAN 9405T resin spline to AL Technologies (S) Pte Ltd in Singapore — an independent third-party test lab — for a 50-year long-term creep test. The results: a short-term flexural modulus of 4,068 MPa, a 50-year projected flexural creep modulus of 1,723 MPa, and a creep factor of 0.43. That's a real, independently-measured, empirical creep retention rate of roughly 43% — the exact number Lesson 39 flagged as missing and had to illustrate with a generic assumed value instead.

What the test actually measured, and how it maps to what this workspace already knew

Working backward from the raw numbers: creep factor (F50) = E50 ÷ E0 = 1,723 ÷ 4,068 ≈ 0.42, which the source rounds to 0.43. Test conditions: a specimen 7.264mm thick, 15.30mm wide, spanning 116mm, loaded with 4.741kg, held for 100 days in 35°C water at pH 6, with modulus back-calculated from deflection at each time interval out to 144,000 minutes (100 days) and then projected to the 50-year mark.

A striking number to notice: 1,723 MPa is almost exactly the 1,724 MPa generic ASTM F1216/OPSS.MUNI flexural modulus floor cited throughout Lessons 22–27 and the certification checklist. That's very unlikely to be a coincidence — the generic code minimum was almost certainly benchmarked against exactly this kind of 50-year creep-projected value in the first place. Seeing a real product's actual 50-year number land right at that historic floor is a genuinely illuminating data point, not just a passing curiosity.

An honest caveat: this is ISO 10468, not literally ASTM D2990 — and the two procedures aren't the same test

The test methodology referenced alongside these results is ISO 10468, "Glass-reinforced thermosetting plastics (GRP) pipes — Determination of the long-term specific ring creep stiffness under wet conditions and calculation of the wet creep factor" (2003 edition; the 2018 and 2023 revisions retitled it to cover both wet and dry conditions). This is the international standard for exactly the same underlying property — long-term creep-retained stiffness — but it is not literally the same document as ASTM D2990, the specific standard OPSS.MUNI and Peel Region's specs name (Lessons 22–23, 39, 41). The two procedures differ in real, specific ways, not just in name:

ASTM D2990ISO 10468
ScopeGeneral-purpose: any plastic material, three loading modes (tensile, compressive, flexural)GRP pipe-specific only — not a general plastics standard
Specimen (flexural mode)A flat rectangular bar, the same shape ASTM D790 uses, loaded in three-point (or optionally four-point, per D6272) bendingTraditionally a curved ring or pipe segment, cut from an actual pipe and loaded diametrally (the same family of specimen ISO/TS 12512 uses for initial ring stiffness) — a fundamentally different geometry from a flat bar
EnvironmentNot inherently wet — run dry unless the test plan specifies otherwiseExplicitly wet-conditioned (full water immersion) in the 2003 edition; later editions allow wet or dry, since dry testing suits raw-material QC while wet testing simulates real buried service conditions
Measurement scheduleDeflection logged at fixed intervals from 1 minute out to 1,000 hours, then extrapolatedDeflection logged over a comparably long real-time hold, then extrapolated to the 50-year design mark using the standard's own wet creep factor calculation
OutputA general creep modulus/strain-vs-time dataset, usable for design comparison across any plastic partA pipe-specific "ring stiffness" and "wet creep factor" figure, built to plug directly into pipe buckling/ring-deflection design equations like F1216's
A specific observation worth flagging about the MERICAN 9405T test itself: the reported specimen — 7.264mm thick, 15.30mm wide, spanning 116mm — describes a flat bar geometry (thickness/width/span), the shape a flexural three-point bend test uses. That's the specimen shape ASTM D2990's flexural-creep method is built around, not the curved ring/pipe-segment specimen ISO 10468's own ring-stiffness methodology is generally associated with. This isn't a confirmed contradiction — test houses sometimes adapt methodology, and the source document may simply be using "ISO 10468" as a label for its underlying wet-creep-factor calculation approach rather than its full ring-specimen procedure — but it's a real, specific reason for caution beyond the "different named standard" point alone. Confirm the actual specimen geometry directly with AL Technologies or Sino Polymer before treating this as a literal ISO 10468 ring test.
What this means practically: this is real, credible, independently-verified long-term performance data — not a fabricated or assumed number. But if a specific bid document requires "ASTM D2990 testing" by name (as OPSS.MUNI 443 and Peel's Appendix 5.9 do), a report using a different specimen geometry and environment may need to be submitted alongside a note on methodological equivalence, or a fresh ASTM D2990 test may still be requested by a specific reviewing engineer. Don't treat this as an automatic substitute for a named-standard requirement — treat it as strong evidence the underlying long-term performance is real and good, worth leading with while confirming the exact document and procedure a given spec wants.

A second, independent regulatory driver for the same 50-year requirement

The test wasn't run for ASTM F1216's sake — it was run to satisfy WIS 4-34-04-1995, a UK Water Industry Specification: "Guiding Standard for the Lining Repair Construction of Drainage Pipes by Hardening Pipes in Place (CIPP)," which requires the flexural modulus retention rate of CIPP-process FRP pipes to reach more than 40% within 50 years. MERICAN 9405T's measured 43% clears that bar directly. This is a genuinely new standard for this workspace — Lesson 14's Europe regulatory survey covered ISO 11296-4 and the EU drinking-water positive lists, but not this specific UK water-industry CIPP spec. Worth logging as a fourth regional standard alongside ASTM F1216 (US), OPSS.MUNI (Ontario), and ISO 11296-4 (EU-wide) — WIS specs are the UK water utility sector's own additional layer on top of the ISO/EN baseline.

Important: "clears WIS" is not the same claim as "clears Peel." Peel Region's own bid specifications set a higher bar than WIS's 40% — ≥50% for hot water/steam liners, ≥65% for UV-cured liners (Lesson 27, primary source). MERICAN 9405T's measured 43% clears WIS's 40% floor, but falls short of Peel's 50% non-reinforced floor and well short of its 65% UV floor. This is exactly the same discipline applied throughout this lesson arc: real evidence that clears one bar doesn't automatically clear a different, unrelated bar just because both concern "creep retention." See Lesson 39's comparison table for every retention figure encountered in this workspace side by side, since they genuinely aren't interchangeable.
One more clarification worth being precise about: neither ASTM D2990 nor ISO 10468 specifies a required retention percentage at all. Both are test methods — they define how to measure long-term creep behavior, not what result counts as passing. The 50%/65% figures are Peel's own requirement, the 40% figure is WIS's own requirement, and the 43% figure is MERICAN 9405T's actual measured result. Don't look to D2990 or ISO 10468 themselves for a "typical" or "required" number — they wouldn't have one to give.

Why this matters beyond one resin

This test result answers three separate open questions that have been tracked since early in this workspace:

  • Does Sino Polymer have any long-term creep data at all? Yes — for at least MERICAN 9405T, real and third-party verified, not just a qualitative claim.
  • What's a realistic creep retention factor for this resin family, rather than a generic textbook assumption? ~43%, empirically measured — a meaningfully more defensible number than the illustrative 50% figure Lesson 39 had to use for lack of anything better.
  • Does the company have in-house capability to generate more of this data on demand? The same source document lists Sino Polymer's own R&D center equipment — FTIR, GPC, DMA, DSC, Karl Fischer titration, TGA, HDT testing, a universal testing machine — real lab capability to run exactly this kind of characterization, not just outsource it opportunistically.

What's still not resolved

This is one test, on one product (MERICAN 9405T), via one methodology (ISO 10468, not ASTM D2990), at one lab. It doesn't automatically extend to every other product in the line, and it's neat-resin/cast-spline data — not the composite (resin + felt/fiberglass) data Lesson 41 flagged as the deeper structural gap. Lesson 43 picks up that second half of the story directly.
