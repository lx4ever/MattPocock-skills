Lesson 36: Getting to the Manhole

Lesson 35 flagged three Birch Cres segments as "Difficult Access" and "Provisional." This is what that actually looks like on the ground — and a genuinely interesting puzzle about which project this document really belongs to.

Appendix 5.25 is a four-sheet property impact plan for exactly the Birch Cres easement segments flagged in Lesson 35: existing conditions, the planned work footprint, seven dated site photos, and construction-access engineering details. It's the physical, on-the-ground counterpart to a repair-list row that just says "Difficult Access."

A puzzle worth being upfront about

This document's title block says "Region of Peel Sewer and Maintenance Hole Rehabilitation Program 2024," Project No. 24-2303B — the same program name and project family as the TRCA permit in Lesson 34, not "Project 23-2300K" (the 2026-142T tender Lessons 26–33 are built on). And yet: the maintenance hole numbers on this plan (MH1785801 through MH1785804) are the exact same MH numbers as the "Difficult Access" segments in Lesson 35's repair list, which explicitly lives inside the 2026-142T package and cites this exact document by name ("Refer to Appendix 5.25 Property Impact Plan on Birch Cres").

The most defensible reading, stated as an inference rather than a confirmed fact: "Sewer and Maintenance Hole Rehabilitation Program 2024" looks like an umbrella engineering design program — the same engineering firm (andrews.engineer) producing property impact plans and permit packages for individual street segments, dated October 2024 — that later got bundled into specific construction tenders as they came up for bid, including both the standalone TRCA-permitted culvert work (Lesson 34) and this Birch Cres segment, which ended up folded into the larger 2026-142T contract. Worth flagging clearly rather than either assuming these are unrelated documents or asserting a certain organizational structure that isn't fully confirmed by what's in front of us.

What "buried maintenance hole" actually means

The plan marks MH1785804 explicitly: "BURIED MH1785804 TO BE EXPOSED AS NEEDED (PROVISIONAL)" — and MH1785803 similarly, "(BURIED; TO BE EXPOSED)." These maintenance holes sit inside a rear-yard easement between properties, not under a road — over time, landscaping, sod, or paving has covered them, and they're not currently visible or accessible at grade. Before any CIPP work can begin at these access points, a crew has to locate and physically excavate down to the existing structure.

Documenting the "before," photographically

Seven dated, geotagged site photos are logged against specific addresses and purposes — "Photo 2: 2625 Birch Cres — wood fence and shed to be removed," "Photo 6: 2591 Birch Cres — MH1785802 (access MH)." This is a real, standard practice worth knowing exists: before disturbing private property, document its exact pre-existing condition with photos tied to addresses. It's a dispute-prevention step — if a homeowner later claims something was damaged that wasn't part of the planned work, the dated photo record settles the question.

How you actually get equipment across a backyard

Two real engineering details govern site access itself:

  • Wooden swamp mats — three-ply bolted Canadian Douglas Fir or Oak, installed on access routes to protect existing vegetation and spread equipment loading over soft ground, used where slope conditions allow.
  • Temporary granular construction access road — used instead where swamp mats aren't suitable: minimum 4,000mm wide, a minimum 300mm depth of 50mm crushed aggregate, over two layers of non-woven geotextile fabric (or steel plate) to protect the existing topsoil underneath. The contractor has to avoid removing existing topsoil before placing the geotextile, and every single piece of the granular fill has to be removed during restoration — the site has to go back to original condition or better, to the Contract Administrator's satisfaction.

Why this belongs in a sales/estimating mission

A quote built purely from ASTM design parameters and pay-item unit prices (Lessons 27–28) would miss every line of the removal-and-reinstatement table above, the swamp-mat/access-road decision, and the buried-MH excavation. On a segment like this, the "easy" technical part — designing and installing the liner itself — can be a smaller share of total cost and schedule risk than the access logistics required just to reach the maintenance hole in the first place.
