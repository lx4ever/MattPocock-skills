Lesson 2: How CIPP Is Installed

Six steps, from a dirty pipe to a client-ready line — plus the one method choice that actually changes the sales conversation.

You now know CIPP is a resin-soaked tube that gets cured hard inside the old pipe. This lesson is the walk-through you'd actually give a client: what happens on site, in order, and where the real decisions get made.

The six steps

  1. Clean & inspect. The line is jetted clean and run with a CCTV camera first. No liner goes in until the crew can see exactly what they're lining and confirm the pipe is clear enough for the resin to bond properly.
  2. Insert the liner — inversion or pull-in-place. The resin-soaked tube gets into the pipe one of two ways (see below). This is the one step with a real trade-off worth knowing.
  3. Calibrate. Air or water pressure presses the liner firmly against the host pipe wall from end to end, working out wrinkles before it hardens. Once it's snug, pressure is held steady through curing.
  4. Cure. The resin is hardened using ambient air, hot water, steam, or UV light — the choice from Lesson 1. This is what turns a soft, wet tube into a hard new pipe wall.
  5. Reinstate the laterals. Curing seals over every branch connection (the pipes running to individual homes or fixtures). A robotic cutter, driven in from a manhole, reopens each one from the inside — no digging at the house end either.
  6. Trim & final inspection. Excess liner at the access points is trimmed flush, and a final CCTV run confirms the new pipe looks right end to end.

Inversion vs. pull-in-place

Step 2 is where CIPP installers make a real call, and it's worth being able to speak to:

InversionPull-in-place
How it goes in Turned inside-out as air or water pressure pushes it through the pipe from one access point, unrolling as it travels Winched through the pipe from one manhole to another using a cable
Access needed One access point Typically two access points
Best for Straightforward runs; pressure presses it evenly against the wall as it goes Tight bends and complex geometries, where inversion is more prone to wrinkling
Sales-relevant point: this is a legitimate reason a contractor might quote one job differently from another with the same pipe material and diameter. If a client asks why, "your run has three tight bends, so we're pulling it in rather than inverting it" is a real, defensible answer — not a stall.
