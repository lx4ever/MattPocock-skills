Lesson 51: The Toronto Tender — How a Real Bid Package Comes Together

A live, $9.7–11.4M City of Toronto CIPP contract, reviewed document by document — the structure every bidder has to work through before price ever gets compared.

Every prior lesson on real procurement in this workspace has come from Peel Region. This is a different municipality, a different document architecture, and — crucially — the first time this workspace has the entire package for one live contract: all five RFT parts, four addenda, and the actual repair list. This lesson is the map before the detail lessons that follow — how the pieces fit together, and where a bidder can get disqualified before their price is ever even compared to anyone else's.

The document stack — five parts, not one file

A "bid package" here isn't a single document — it's five parts plus whatever addenda land before the deadline:

  • Part 1 — Request for Tenders Process: submission rules, evaluation method, the City's reserved rights, definitions. The procedural rulebook, not the technical spec.
  • Part 2 — Construction Agreement: the actual contract the winning bidder signs — General Conditions, insurance, bonds, holdback, warranty, dispute resolution.
  • Part 3 — Drawings and Specifications: 196 pages. The real technical content — TS 4.10 (Sewer CIPP) and TS 466 (Sewer CIPPSR) amendments, the pay-item/pricing structure, permits, notices, and site-specific special provisions.
  • Part 4 — Forms: the actual submission documents — Form B (Bid Bond), Form C (Experience and Qualifications), Form D (List of Subcontractors), Form E (CIPP/CIPPSR Material Properties).
  • Part 5 — Instructions to Pricing Form: one page. The real pricing form itself lives inside the City's online procurement system (SAP Ariba), not as a standalone document.
A concrete, worth-remembering fact: Form E — the material properties form every resin supplier's submission depends on — wasn't in the original package at all. It was added via Addendum 2, three weeks into the tender period, after a bidder asked where it was. A bid built only from the Part 1-5 documents issued January 17 would have missed a mandatory submission requirement entirely. Watching every addendum until the submission deadline isn't due diligence — it's the only way to know the actual current requirements.

Two-stage evaluation, and why it's simpler than it looks

Section 2.1 of Part 1 spells out exactly two stages:

  1. Stage 1 — compliance gate. Bids are checked against every mandatory requirement (Section 1.9, Submission of Bids). Substantially incomplete or non-compliant bids are excluded here, before price is even considered.
  2. Stage 2 — price ranking. Every bid that survives Stage 1 is ranked purely on lowest total submitted pricing. There is no weighted technical score, no qualitative evaluation of approach — compliant bids compete on price alone.
This changes what "winning the bid" actually means. There's no partial credit for a strong technical submission if the price isn't competitive, and there's no way to out-price a competitor if the bid is non-compliant — Form E left blank, an addendum not acknowledged, a "TBD" where a number was required. The entire competitive contest happens inside the group of bids that already cleared every mandatory requirement. Getting the paperwork exactly right isn't a formality here — it's the precondition for the price competition to matter at all.

The experience bar that filters bidders before price matters at all

Form C sets a real, quantified qualification floor — not a general "relevant experience" statement:

Minimum 12 kilometres of CIPP lining in gravity sewer applications with similar requirements, completed as the General Contractor (not as a subcontractor), across at least 3 projects in the past 5 years, each project a minimum $5 million CAD (excluding taxes) in value, and each completed on schedule. The experience must be the bidding entity's own corporate experience — not a named individual's, not a subcontractor's borrowed track record.

This is a real bar most small or newly-formed CIPP contractors can't clear on their own — which is exactly the mechanism it's designed to be. A resin supplier or smaller specialist contractor without this history has a real, structural reason to pursue this kind of work as a subcontractor (Form D lists "Sewer CIPP" and "Sewer CIPPSR" as their own distinct subcontractable work types) rather than as the prime bidder.

Submission is entirely digital — and "close enough" isn't compliant

Every bid goes through SAP Ariba (the City Online Procurement System) — no paper, email, or fax submission is accepted. The Bid Bond specifically must be a digital e-bond, authenticated and verifiable; a scanned paper bond doesn't qualify. Part 5's own pricing instructions are explicit: leaving a line blank, or entering "n/a," "-," "tba," or "included" instead of an actual number, gets the bid declared non-compliant outright. Zero-cost items still have to be entered as "$0.00" — silence isn't the same as zero.

How this compares to Peel Region's process

This workspace's earlier procurement lessons (26–37) were built entirely around Peel Region's bid documents. The core technical thread — F1216 design methodology, OPSS.MUNI 443 materials requirements — carries over directly (Lessons 38–41, 49–50 apply to both). But the procurement mechanics differ in real, concrete ways: Toronto's evaluation is a strict two-stage compliance-then-price process with no weighted technical scoring, its experience bar is expressed in kilometres and dollar value rather than the narrower "Ontario installation history" question Peel raised (Lesson 26), and its submission platform (Ariba) and digital bonding requirements are its own. Treat Peel's and Toronto's processes as two real, independently-verified examples of Ontario municipal CIPP procurement — not as interchangeable templates for each other.
